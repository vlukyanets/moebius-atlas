## Why It Matters

"Through two points passes exactly one line" - nobody proved that; it
was simply agreed. "The sum of the angles of a triangle is $180^\circ$" -
that one *can* be proved, and will be. What's the difference, and why
does it matter? Because geometry is a building: a few agreed
foundations, everything else stacked on them by reasoning. Knowing
which statements are foundations, which are proved, and which are
just names is how you tell a proof from a guess.

## The Big Idea

You can't prove everything - a proof rests on earlier facts, and
those on earlier ones, and the chain has to start somewhere. The
starting facts are axioms: statements accepted without proof, chosen
because they're simple and obviously true of points and lines. The
properties of belonging, of measuring segments and angles, are
axioms.

Everything else is a theorem: a statement that must be deduced from
axioms and earlier theorems by a chain of reasoning - a proof. And a
definition is neither; it's a name given to a figure or a property
("the bisector is the ray that ...") so that it can be talked about.
Definitions aren't true or false; they're agreements about words.

## The Rule

An **axiom** is a statement accepted as true without proof. The
axioms of planimetry so far:

- the properties of belonging of points and lines;
- the properties of measuring segments and angles;
- the property of laying off a segment of given length on a ray from
  its origin, and an angle of given measure from a ray on a given
  side - each in exactly one way.

A **theorem** is a statement whose truth is established by reasoning.
A **proof** is that reasoning: a chain of steps, each justified by an
axiom, a definition, or a theorem already proved. A theorem has a
**hypothesis** (what is given) and a **conclusion** (what is to be
proved); "if ... then ..." makes them explicit.

A **definition** explains the meaning of a new term through terms
already known.

Swapping hypothesis and conclusion gives the **converse** statement;
it may or may not be true and needs its own proof.

## Worked Example

Sort these: "Through two points passes exactly one line." "Two
distinct lines have at most one common point." "The bisector of an
angle is the ray dividing it into two equal angles." And state the
converse of "if two angles are equal, their measures are equal."

- **"Through two points ..."** - an axiom: accepted, not proved.
- **"Two lines have at most one common point"** - a theorem. Proof: if
  they had two common points, both would pass through those two
  points; by the axiom only one line does, so they'd be the same line,
  not distinct. That short chain from the axiom *is* the proof.
- **"The bisector is ..."** - a definition: it names a ray, it asserts
  nothing.
- **Converse:** "if the measures of two angles are equal, the angles
  are equal." That happens to be true too - it's the definition of
  equal angles read backwards.

A converse that fails: "if a number is divisible by $4$, it's even" is
true; "if a number is even, it's divisible by $4$" is false - $6$.

## Common Mistake

Proving a statement by measuring a drawing, or by citing the
statement itself. A protractor reading is evidence about one picture,
not a proof about all triangles. And "the angles are equal because
they're equal" - restating the conclusion - proves nothing. Each step
must lean on an axiom, a definition or a previous theorem, and you
should be able to name which.

## The Takeaway

Axioms are accepted, theorems are proved, definitions are names. A
proof is a chain from the given to the claim, each link justified. The
converse of a theorem swaps given and claim and needs checking on its
own.

## Check Yourself

1. Axiom, theorem or definition: "a straight angle measures
   $180^\circ$"?
2. Axiom, theorem or definition: "a right angle is an angle of
   $90^\circ$"?
3. State the hypothesis and conclusion of "if a point lies between two
   others, the two segments add up to the whole."
4. State the converse of "if two segments are equal, their lengths are
   equal." Is it true?
5. Why can't every statement in geometry be proved?
6. Someone "proves" that two lines meet at most once by drawing two
   lines and counting. Why isn't that a proof?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. An axiom (part of the measurement properties).
> 2. A definition.
> 3. Hypothesis: a point lies between two others. Conclusion: the two
>    segments sum to the whole.
> 4. "If two segments have equal lengths, they are equal." True - by
>    definition.
> 5. Every proof relies on earlier statements; the chain must start from
>    accepted ones.
> 6. A drawing shows one case; a proof must cover all lines, by reasoning
>    from the axioms.
