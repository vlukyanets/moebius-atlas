## Why It Matters

A laser pointer switched on in a dark room: the beam starts at the pointer
and goes on until it hits something - and if nothing were in the way, it
would go on forever. A railway track on a flat plain, running off in both
directions past the horizon. Neither of these is a segment: they don't
have two ends. Geometry needs names for "straight and never-ending," and
this is where it gets them.

## The Big Idea

Take a segment and let one end run away to infinity - you get a ray: a
start, but no finish. Let both ends run away and you get a line: no start
and no finish, straight in both directions, forever. On paper you can only
ever draw a piece of a ray or a line, so the arrowless ends are a promise:
"this keeps going."

Points and lines obey one simple fact that everything else rests on:
through any two points you can draw exactly one line. Two pins and a
ruler - there's only one way to lay the ruler against both.

## The Rule

A **line** is straight and has no ends - it extends without limit in both
directions. It's named by two of its points, like the line $AB$, or by one
small letter, like the line $m$.

A **ray** is the part of a line on one side of a point of it, together
with that point. The point is the ray's **origin**; the ray with origin
$O$ passing through $A$ is written $OA$ - origin first, always. The rays
$OA$ and $AO$ are different rays.

A point of a line splits it into two rays with the same origin. Two such
rays pointing opposite ways are called **opposite rays**, and together
they make up the whole line.

Through any two points passes exactly one line. Two different lines can
share at most one point - where they **intersect** - or none at all.

A **plane** is the flat surface all of this is drawn on: like a
tabletop, but without edges, extending without limit in every direction.

## Worked Example

Mark points $A$, $B$ and $C$ not on one line. How many lines can be drawn
through pairs of them, and how many rays with origin $A$ go through the
other points?

- **Lines.** Pick pairs: $A$ and $B$, $A$ and $C$, $B$ and $C$. Each pair
  gives exactly one line, and since the three points aren't in a row, the
  three lines are all different. So $3$ lines.
- **Rays from $A$.** Ray $AB$ starts at $A$ and runs through $B$; ray
  $AC$ starts at $A$ and runs through $C$. So $2$ rays.
- **Check the naming.** $BA$ would be a different ray - it starts at $B$.

So: $3$ lines, $2$ rays with origin $A$.

## Common Mistake

Swapping the letters of a ray. The segment $AB$ and the segment $BA$ are
one and the same thing. But the ray $AB$ starts at $A$ and goes past $B$
forever, while the ray $BA$ starts at $B$ and goes past $A$ the other way -
they overlap only on the segment between $A$ and $B$. For a ray, the
first letter is the origin, and the order matters.

## The Takeaway

A segment has two ends, a ray has one, a line has none. Through two
points there's exactly one line, and a point on a line cuts it into two
opposite rays.

## Check Yourself

1. Which of these has exactly one end: a segment, a ray, a line?
2. Point $O$ lies on the line $AB$, between $A$ and $B$. Name the two
   opposite rays with origin $O$.
3. How many lines pass through two given points? Through one given
   point?
4. Can two different lines have two common points? Why?
5. Points $A$, $B$, $C$ lie on one line, in that order. Is the ray $BA$
   the same as the ray $BC$?
6. Someone says "the ray $MN$ and the ray $NM$ are the same, just like
   the segments $MN$ and $NM$." Where's the mistake?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. A ray.
> 2. The rays $OA$ and $OB$.
> 3. Exactly one; through a single point, as many as you like.
> 4. No - through two points there's only one line, so they'd be the same
>    line.
> 5. No - they point in opposite directions from $B$; they're opposite
>    rays.
> 6. The segments are the same, but the rays have different origins ($M$
>    and $N$) and point opposite ways.
