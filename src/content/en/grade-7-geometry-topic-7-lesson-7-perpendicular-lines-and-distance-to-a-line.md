## Why It Matters

You met perpendicular lines in grade 6 as lines that cross "square
on." Now they get a proper definition through the angle between
lines, a theorem that there's only one perpendicular through a point,
and a precise meaning for "the distance from a point to a line." That
distance is what every height in a triangle, every altitude and every
"how far from the road" measurement is.

## The Big Idea

Two lines are perpendicular when the angle between them is $90^\circ$ -
and by the vertical angles theorem, one right angle at the crossing
makes all four right. From a point off a line, drop a segment to the
line at a right angle: that's the perpendicular, and its length is the
distance. Any other segment from the point to the line leans, and a
leaning segment is longer - proved properly later, but the picture is
already clear: the straight-down path is the shortest.

Through a given point there's exactly one perpendicular to a line. Two
would make a triangle with two right angles, which can't exist - a
fact this topic states and a later one proves.

## The Rule

Two lines are **perpendicular** if they intersect at a right angle:
$a \perp b$. Then all four angles at the intersection are right.

**Theorem.** Through any point of a line, exactly one line
perpendicular to it can be drawn. (Through a point *not* on the line,
also exactly one - proved once triangles are available.)

The **perpendicular** from a point $A$ to a line $a$ is the segment $AH$
with $H$ on $a$ and $AH \perp a$; $H$ is its **foot**. The **distance from
the point to the line** is the length of the perpendicular.

Any other segment from $A$ to the line is a **slant** (oblique), and it
is longer than the perpendicular.

**Perpendicular bisector** of a segment: the line through its
midpoint perpendicular to it.

## Worked Example

The lines $AB$ and $CD$ meet at $O$, and $\angle AOC = 90^\circ$. Show $AB
\perp CD$ and find $\angle BOD$. Then: from a point $M$ the perpendicular
to a line is $6$ cm and a slant is $10$ cm - what is the distance from
$M$ to the line, and which segment is it?

- **$\angle AOC = 90^\circ$** is one of the four angles at $O$.
- **Its vertical angle** $\angle BOD = 90^\circ$ by the vertical angles
  theorem.
- **Its adjacent angles** $\angle AOD$ and $\angle COB$ are $180^\circ -
  90^\circ = 90^\circ$.
- **So all four are right:** $AB \perp CD$, and $\angle BOD = 90^\circ$.
- **Distance:** the perpendicular, $6$ cm. The $10$ cm slant is longer,
  as any slant is.

Construction: to drop a perpendicular from $M$ to $a$ with a set
square, slide one leg along $a$ until the other leg passes through $M$
and draw it.

## Common Mistake

Measuring "the distance from a point to a line" along whatever
segment is drawn. If the segment isn't at a right angle to the line,
it's a slant, and it overstates the distance. Also: "perpendicular"
is a relation between *lines* (or a segment and a line); a single
segment isn't perpendicular by itself - it's perpendicular *to*
something.

## The Takeaway

Perpendicular lines meet at right angles - one right angle makes all
four. Through a point there's exactly one perpendicular to a line; the
perpendicular segment from a point to a line is the shortest, and its
length is the distance from the point to the line.

## Check Yourself

1. Lines $a$ and $b$ meet, and one angle is $90^\circ$. What are the
   others?
2. From $P$ to a line: perpendicular $4$ cm, slants $5$ cm and $7$ cm.
   What's the distance from $P$ to the line?
3. Can a slant from a point be shorter than the perpendicular from it?
4. How many perpendiculars to a line pass through a point on it? Off
   it?
5. Where is the foot of the perpendicular from a point that lies on the
   line?
6. Someone says the distance from $M$ to $a$ is $10$ cm, measured along a
   slant. What is it really, given the perpendicular is $6$ cm?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. All $90^\circ$.
> 2. $4$ cm.
> 3. No - the perpendicular is the shortest.
> 4. One; one.
> 5. The point itself; the distance is $0$.
> 6. $6$ cm - distance is measured along the perpendicular.
