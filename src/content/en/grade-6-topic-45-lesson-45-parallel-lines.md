## Why It Matters

Railway tracks, the edges of a ruler, the lines on a page, the two
kerbs of a straight road - pairs of lines that run side by side and
never meet, however far they go. If the rails ever met, the train would
derail; if the lines on the page ever met, the writing would collide.
Lines that keep their distance are parallel, and half of geometry is
built on them.

## The Big Idea

Two lines in a plane either cross somewhere or they don't. If they
don't cross - not on the page, not off the edge, not a million
kilometres away - they're parallel. They keep exactly the same distance
apart all the way along, like the two rails of a track: measure across
with a perpendicular anywhere, and you get the same number.

The neatest way to make parallel lines is with perpendiculars. Draw a
line, then two lines perpendicular to it: those two are parallel to
each other - both of them make right angles with the same line, so
neither can lean toward the other. That's also how a set square and a
ruler draw them.

## The Rule

Two lines in a plane are **parallel** if they do not intersect - they
have no common point. Written $a \parallel b$.

Two segments (or rays) are parallel if they lie on parallel lines.

**Through a point not on a given line, exactly one line can be drawn
parallel to the given line.**

If two lines are both perpendicular to a third line, they are parallel
to each other: $a \perp c$ and $b \perp c$ give $a \parallel b$.

The distance between parallel lines - the length of a perpendicular
from any point of one to the other - is the same everywhere along
them.

To draw a line parallel to $a$ through a point $M$ with a set square and
ruler: lay the ruler along $a$, put a leg of the set square against it,
slide the set square along the ruler until its other leg reaches $M$,
and draw.

## Worked Example

The lines $a$ and $b$ are both perpendicular to the line $c$. Are $a$ and
$b$ parallel? Then: a rectangle $ABCD$ - which of its sides are parallel,
and which perpendicular?

- **$a \perp c$ and $b \perp c$.** Both make right angles with $c$. If $a$
  and $b$ met at some point, we'd have two different perpendiculars to
  $c$ through that point - impossible, since there's only one. So
  $a \parallel b$.
- **The rectangle.** All angles are right, so each side is perpendicular
  to its neighbours: $AB \perp BC$, $BC \perp CD$, and so on. Opposite
  sides are both perpendicular to the same side, so they're parallel:
  $AB \parallel CD$ and $BC \parallel AD$.

And the rails: two rails $1.5$ m apart at the station are still $1.5$ m
apart $10$ km later - the distance between parallel lines doesn't
change.

## Common Mistake

Deciding two lines are parallel because they don't cross *on the
page*. Two lines that lean toward each other even slightly will cross
somewhere - maybe far off the paper - and they aren't parallel. Being
parallel is about direction, not about the piece you can see. Check
with a perpendicular: if the distance across is the same at two
different places, the lines are parallel.

## The Takeaway

Parallel lines never meet and stay the same distance apart. Through a
point there's exactly one parallel to a given line, and two lines
perpendicular to the same line are parallel to each other.

## Check Yourself

1. Can two parallel lines have exactly one common point? None? Two?
2. Name three pairs of parallel lines you can see around you.
3. $a \perp c$ and $b \perp c$. What can you say about $a$ and $b$?
4. $a \parallel b$ and $c \perp a$. Is $c \perp b$? Why?
5. In a square $KLMN$, name the side parallel to $KL$ and the sides
   perpendicular to it.
6. Someone says two lines on a page are parallel "because they don't
   cross anywhere on the page." Why is that not enough?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. No; yes; no - parallel means no common point at all.
> 2. For example: opposite edges of a door, the lines on ruled paper,
>    railway rails.
> 3. $a \parallel b$.
> 4. Yes - $c$ makes a right angle with $a$, and $b$ runs in the same
>    direction as $a$.
> 5. $MN$; $LM$ and $NK$.
> 6. They might meet beyond the page; parallel lines never meet anywhere,
>    which shows as equal distance apart everywhere.
