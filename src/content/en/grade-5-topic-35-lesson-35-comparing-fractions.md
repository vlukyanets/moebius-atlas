## Why It Matters

Two pizzas the same size, both cut into $8$. You've eaten $3$ slices of
one and your friend $5$ of the other - who ate more? Nobody has to think
about that: five slices beat three. When the slices are the same size,
comparing fractions is just comparing how many slices - and that's the
whole of this topic.

## The Big Idea

The denominator names the size of the piece. Two fractions with the same
denominator are made of the same-sized pieces, so the only question is
who has more pieces - the numerator settles it. $\frac{5}{8}$ beats
$\frac{3}{8}$ for the same reason five apples beat three apples.

On the coordinate ray, that's obvious to the eye: with the unit split
into eighths, $\frac{3}{8}$ is three steps along and $\frac{5}{8}$ is
five - and the point further right is the bigger number, exactly as it
was for natural numbers.

## The Rule

**Of two fractions with the same denominator, the one with the bigger
numerator is bigger:**

$$
\frac{3}{8} < \frac{5}{8} \quad \text{because } 3 < 5
$$

Fractions with the same denominator and the same numerator are equal.

Comparing with $1$ and with $0$ needs no partner: a fraction whose
numerator is smaller than its denominator is less than $1$; one whose
numerator equals its denominator is exactly $1$; and a fraction with
numerator $0$ is $0$.

On the coordinate ray, the bigger fraction sits further from the origin.

## Worked Example

Put in order from smallest to largest: $\frac{7}{10}$, $\frac{3}{10}$,
$\frac{9}{10}$, $\frac{10}{10}$.

- **Same denominator everywhere** - tenths - so only the numerators
  matter.
- **Order the numerators:** $3 < 7 < 9 < 10$.
- **Write the fractions in that order:** $\frac{3}{10} < \frac{7}{10} <
  \frac{9}{10} < \frac{10}{10}$.
- **Notice the last one:** $\frac{10}{10}$ is ten tenths, the whole thing -
  it equals $1$.

Now the pizza: $\frac{3}{8}$ against $\frac{5}{8}$. $3 < 5$, so
$\frac{3}{8} < \frac{5}{8}$ - the friend ate more.

## Common Mistake

Comparing the denominators when they're the same - or, worse, applying
this rule when they're *not*. $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{3}{8}$ both have
numerator $3$, but they're not equal: a quarter is a bigger piece than an
eighth, so $\frac{3}{4}$ is bigger. The rule on this page only works for
same-sized pieces. Different denominators mean different piece sizes,
and that comparison needs a different tool, which comes later.

## The Takeaway

Same denominator means same-sized pieces, so the fraction with more
pieces - the bigger numerator - is the bigger fraction. On the number
line it's simply the one further right.

## Check Yourself

1. Put in $<$, $>$ or $=$: $\frac{4}{9}$ and $\frac{7}{9}$.
2. Put in $<$, $>$ or $=$: $\frac{11}{15}$ and $\frac{11}{15}$.
3. Write in order from largest to smallest: $\frac{2}{7}$, $\frac{6}{7}$,
   $\frac{5}{7}$, $\frac{1}{7}$.
4. Which fractions with denominator $6$ are less than $\frac{4}{6}$?
5. Which is bigger, $\frac{12}{12}$ or $\frac{11}{12}$? What is
   $\frac{12}{12}$?
6. Someone says $\frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{5}$ "because the numerators are
   the same." What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $\frac{4}{9} < \frac{7}{9}$.
> 2. Equal.
> 3. $\frac{6}{7} > \frac{5}{7} > \frac{2}{7} > \frac{1}{7}$.
> 4. $\frac{1}{6}$, $\frac{2}{6}$, $\frac{3}{6}$.
> 5. $\frac{12}{12}$, which is $1$.
> 6. The denominators differ, so the pieces are different sizes; a third
>    is bigger than a fifth, so $\frac{2}{3} > \frac{2}{5}$.
