## Why It Matters

Walls meet floors, streets cross at junctions, the lines on graph paper
cross each other - and in every case, they meet "square on." Buildings
that don't stand square fall down; maps that aren't drawn square
mislead. The right angle is the most useful angle there is, and two
lines that make it have a name of their own.

## The Big Idea

Two lines crossing make four angles. Usually two are acute and two are
obtuse. But there's one special way to cross: when all four are the
same - and then each is a quarter turn, a right angle. Lines that cross
like that are perpendicular. The corner of a page, the plus sign, the
cross-hairs of a sight are all perpendicular pairs.

Through any point you can draw exactly one line perpendicular to a
given line - a set square does it in one move. And the segment along
that perpendicular, from the point to the line, is the shortest route
from the point to the line: any other path is a slanted one and longer.

## The Rule

Two lines are **perpendicular** if they intersect at a right angle. If
all four angles at the intersection are then right angles, it's enough
that one of them is - the others follow. Written $a \perp b$, read
"$a$ is perpendicular to $b$."

Segments and rays are perpendicular if they lie on perpendicular lines:
$AB \perp CD$.

**Through a given point, exactly one line can be drawn perpendicular to
a given line.**

The **perpendicular from a point to a line** is the segment from the
point to the line along that perpendicular; its length is the
**distance from the point to the line** - the shortest of all segments
joining them.

To draw a perpendicular with a set square: lay one leg of the right
angle along the line, slide it until the other leg passes through the
point, and draw along that leg.

## Worked Example

The lines $AB$ and $CD$ intersect at $O$, and $\angle AOC = 90^\circ$. Find
the other three angles and say what $AB$ and $CD$ are to each other.

- **$\angle AOC = 90^\circ$** is given.
- **$\angle AOD$** lies along the straight line $CD$ with $\angle AOC$, so
  together they make $180^\circ$: $\angle AOD = 90^\circ$.
- **$\angle BOD$ and $\angle BOC$** likewise pair with $90^\circ$ angles
  along straight lines: both $90^\circ$.
- **Conclusion:** all four are right angles, so $AB \perp CD$.

Now the distance. A point $M$ is $5$ cm from the line $a$ along the
perpendicular, and a slanted segment from $M$ to $a$ is $7$ cm. Which is
the distance from $M$ to $a$? The perpendicular: $5$ cm. The slanted
segment is always longer.

## Common Mistake

Calling a slanted segment "the distance" because it happens to reach
the line. The distance from a point to a line is measured along the
perpendicular - and only there. Any segment that leans is longer, so
it isn't the distance. If you're asked how far a point is from a line,
draw the perpendicular first.

## The Takeaway

Perpendicular lines cross at right angles - one right angle at the
crossing makes all four. Through a point there's exactly one
perpendicular to a line, and its segment to the line is the shortest
route, which is what "distance from a point to a line" means.

## Check Yourself

1. Two lines cross and one of the angles is $90^\circ$. What are the other
   three?
2. Name three perpendicular pairs in a classroom.
3. How many lines perpendicular to a given line pass through a given
   point?
4. A point is joined to a line by segments of $4$ cm, $6$ cm and $9$ cm,
   one of them the perpendicular. Which one, and what's the distance?
5. Is the diagonal of a square perpendicular to its side? Why not?
6. Someone says "the distance from $M$ to $a$ is $7$ cm, I measured it
   along a slanted segment." What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. All $90^\circ$.
> 2. For example: wall and floor, the two edges of a door at a corner,
>    the lines on graph paper.
> 3. Exactly one.
> 4. The $4$ cm one - the perpendicular is the shortest; the distance is
>    $4$ cm.
> 5. No - it makes $45^\circ$ angles with the sides, not $90^\circ$.
> 6. Distance is measured along the perpendicular, which is shorter than
>    any slanted segment; $7$ cm is an overestimate.
