## Why It Matters

A bucket seen from the side, a lampshade, the cross-section of a dam,
a table leaf - four sides with just *one* pair parallel. That's a
trapezoid, and it's what you get when a parallelogram's symmetry is
halved. It's the last of the basic quadrilaterals, it has its own
vocabulary and its own special case, and next year its area formula
will be one of the three that matter.

## The Big Idea

Two sides parallel, two not. The parallel ones are the bases - think
of the trapezoid sitting on the longer one; the other two are the legs.
Because the bases are parallel, each leg is a transversal, and the two
angles at each leg are co-interior: they sum to $180^\circ$. That's the
one angle fact every trapezoid has.

If the legs are equal, the trapezoid is isosceles, and it behaves like
an isosceles triangle with its top sliced off: the base angles are
equal and the diagonals are equal. Drop perpendiculars from the ends
of the short base to the long one, and the long base splits into a
rectangle's width plus two equal pieces - the standard way to compute
anything about an isosceles trapezoid.

## The Rule

A **trapezoid** is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel
sides. The parallel sides are the **bases**, the other two the
**legs**. The **altitude** is a perpendicular between the bases.

**Property.** The angles at each leg sum to $180^\circ$ (co-interior
angles on parallel lines).

A trapezoid is **isosceles** if its legs are equal, and **right** if a
leg is perpendicular to the bases.

**Theorem.** In an isosceles trapezoid, the angles at each base are
equal, and the diagonals are equal.

*Idea of proof.* Through one end of the short base draw a line
parallel to the far leg; it cuts off a parallelogram and an isosceles
triangle, whose base angles give the equal angles at the long base.
The diagonals are then equal by SAS on the two triangles they form
with the long base. $\square$

**Converse.** A trapezoid with equal base angles is isosceles.

## Worked Example

In trapezoid $ABCD$ with bases $AD$ and $BC$, $\angle A = 70^\circ$ and $\angle
C = 120^\circ$. Find $\angle B$ and $\angle D$. Then: an isosceles trapezoid has
bases $16$ and $6$ and legs $13$ - find its altitude (the $5, 12, 13$
triangle).

- **Angles at leg $AB$:** $\angle A + \angle B = 180^\circ$, so $\angle B =
  110^\circ$.
- **Angles at leg $CD$:** $\angle C + \angle D = 180^\circ$, so $\angle D = 60^\circ$.
  Check: $70 + 110 + 120 + 60 = 360$.
- **Altitude.** Drop perpendiculars from the ends of the short base
  $BC$ to $AD$: the middle piece is $6$, the two outer pieces are equal,
  $(16 - 6) : 2 = 5$ each. A leg, a piece of $5$ and the altitude form a
  right triangle with hypotenuse $13$: the altitude is $12$.

An isosceles trapezoid with $\angle A = 70^\circ$ has $\angle D = 70^\circ$ and
$\angle B = \angle C = 110^\circ$.

## Common Mistake

Using parallelogram properties on a trapezoid. Its opposite sides are
*not* equal (except possibly the legs), its opposite angles are *not*
equal, and its diagonals do *not* bisect each other. The only angle
rule is the $180^\circ$ at each leg - between the two angles that share
a leg, not across the figure. And "isosceles" refers to the *legs*
being equal; equal bases would make it a parallelogram.

## The Takeaway

One pair of parallel sides: bases and legs, angles at each leg summing
to $180^\circ$. Equal legs make it isosceles, with equal base angles and
equal diagonals; perpendiculars from the short base split the long one
into equal end pieces, which is how its numbers are found.

## Check Yourself

1. In trapezoid $ABCD$ ($AD \parallel BC$), $\angle B = 125^\circ$. Find $\angle
   A$.
2. An isosceles trapezoid has an angle of $65^\circ$. Find the other
   three.
3. An isosceles trapezoid has bases $20$ and $8$. Into what pieces do
   the perpendiculars from the short base divide the long one?
4. A right trapezoid has angles $90^\circ$, $90^\circ$ and $130^\circ$. Find
   the fourth.
5. Can a trapezoid have two right angles? Where are they?
6. Someone says the diagonals of a trapezoid bisect each other, "like a
   parallelogram." Why not?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $55^\circ$.
> 2. $65^\circ$, $115^\circ$, $115^\circ$.
> 3. $6$, $8$, $6$.
> 4. $50^\circ$.
> 5. Yes - a right trapezoid, with both right angles at the ends of the
>    leg perpendicular to the bases.
> 6. That property needs both pairs of sides parallel; with one pair,
>    the triangles at the crossing aren't congruent.
