## Why It Matters

One board is $2\frac{3}{4}$ m long and another is $1\frac{2}{4}$ m - laid
end to end, how long are they? A jug held $3\frac{1}{5}$ litres and
$1\frac{3}{5}$ was poured out - what's left? Real measurements come as
wholes and parts together, and adding or subtracting them shouldn't
mean converting everything to a heap of fifths first. It doesn't: wholes
go with wholes, parts go with parts.

## The Big Idea

A mixed number is a sum, $2\frac{3}{4} = 2 + \frac{3}{4}$. So adding two
mixed numbers is adding four things, and you can group them any way you
like - the commutative and associative properties allow it. Group the
wholes together and the fractions together: $(2 + 1) + (\frac{3}{4} +
\frac{2}{4})$. Two easy sums instead of one awkward one.

Sometimes the fraction parts add up to more than a whole - $\frac{3}{4} +
\frac{2}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$, which is $1\frac{1}{4}$. That extra $1$ gets
carried over to the wholes, exactly like a carry in column addition.
Subtraction has the mirror image: if the fraction part you're taking
away is bigger than the one you have, you borrow a whole and break it
into pieces first.

## The Rule

**To add mixed numbers:** add the whole parts, add the fractional parts,
put them together. If the fractional part comes out improper, carry the
extra whole into the whole part.

$$
2\frac{3}{4} + 1\frac{2}{4} = (2 + 1) + \frac{3 + 2}{4} = 3\frac{5}{4} =
4\frac{1}{4}
$$

**To subtract mixed numbers:** subtract the whole parts, subtract the
fractional parts. If the fractional part on top is too small, borrow $1$
from its whole part and turn it into "denominator over denominator" so
the subtraction goes through.

$$
3\frac{1}{5} - 1\frac{3}{5} = 2\frac{6}{5} - 1\frac{3}{5} = 1\frac{3}{5}
$$

Here $3\frac{1}{5}$ became $2 + \frac{5}{5} + \frac{1}{5} = 2\frac{6}{5}$
before the subtraction.

The other route always works too: turn both into improper fractions, add
or subtract, and convert back.

## Worked Example

Find $5\frac{7}{9} - 2\frac{8}{9}$.

- **Compare the fraction parts.** $\frac{7}{9}$ on top, $\frac{8}{9}$
  below - too small to subtract from. Borrow.
- **Borrow a whole:** $5\frac{7}{9} = 4 + \frac{9}{9} + \frac{7}{9} =
  4\frac{16}{9}$.
- **Now subtract wholes and parts:** $4 - 2 = 2$ and $\frac{16}{9} -
  \frac{8}{9} = \frac{8}{9}$.

So $5\frac{7}{9} - 2\frac{8}{9} = 2\frac{8}{9}$. Check by adding back:
$2\frac{8}{9} + 2\frac{8}{9} = 4\frac{16}{9} = 5\frac{7}{9}$.

Now the boards: $2\frac{3}{4} + 1\frac{2}{4}$. Wholes $2 + 1 = 3$; parts
$\frac{3}{4} + \frac{2}{4} = \frac{5}{4} = 1\frac{1}{4}$; together $3 +
1\frac{1}{4} = 4\frac{1}{4}$ m.

## Common Mistake

Subtracting the fraction parts "the way that works," small from big.
In $5\frac{7}{9} - 2\frac{8}{9}$, someone computes $\frac{8}{9} -
\frac{7}{9} = \frac{1}{9}$ and answers $3\frac{1}{9}$. But the top number
is what you subtract *from*, always - the same rule as in column
subtraction, where a small digit on top means borrow, not swap. The
right answer, $2\frac{8}{9}$, is smaller than the wrong one by a whole
$\frac{2}{9}$.

## The Takeaway

Add wholes with wholes and parts with parts, carrying a whole up when the
parts overflow. Subtract the same way, borrowing a whole down into
"denominator over denominator" when the top part is too small.

## Check Yourself

1. Find $3\frac{2}{7} + 4\frac{3}{7}$.
2. Find $1\frac{5}{6} + 2\frac{4}{6}$.
3. Find $6\frac{4}{5} - 2\frac{1}{5}$.
4. Find $4\frac{1}{3} - 1\frac{2}{3}$.
5. Find $5 - 2\frac{3}{8}$.
6. Someone finds $5\frac{7}{9} - 2\frac{8}{9} = 3\frac{1}{9}$. Where's the
   mistake, and what's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $7\frac{5}{7}$.
> 2. $3\frac{9}{6} = 4\frac{3}{6}$.
> 3. $4\frac{3}{5}$.
> 4. $3\frac{4}{3} - 1\frac{2}{3} = 2\frac{2}{3}$.
> 5. $4\frac{8}{8} - 2\frac{3}{8} = 2\frac{5}{8}$.
> 6. The fraction parts were swapped; borrowing gives $4\frac{16}{9} -
>    2\frac{8}{9} = 2\frac{8}{9}$.
