## Why It Matters

Fold a triangle with two equal sides along the line between them, and
the two halves match: the base angles land on each other. That
symmetry is the first real theorem proved with a congruence
criterion, and it runs both ways - equal sides give equal base angles,
and equal base angles give equal sides. It's the tool for a whole
class of angle-chasing problems, and every roof gable, every A-frame,
uses it.

## The Big Idea

Take $\triangle ABC$ with $AB = AC$. Compare it with itself, but read
backwards: $\triangle ABC$ against $\triangle ACB$. Two sides match ($AB
= AC$ and $AC = AB$) and the angle between them is the same angle $A$.
By SAS the two readings are congruent - and the correspondence sends
$\angle B$ to $\angle C$. So the base angles are equal. No extra lines
needed: the triangle is compared with its own mirror image.

The converse - equal base angles give equal sides - uses the same
trick with ASA. Together they say: a triangle is isosceles exactly
when two of its angles are equal.

## The Rule

A triangle is **isosceles** if two of its sides are equal; they are
the **legs** (lateral sides), the third is the **base**, and the
angles at the base are the **base angles**. An **equilateral**
triangle has all three sides equal.

**Theorem.** In an isosceles triangle the base angles are equal.

*Proof.* Let $AB = AC$. Consider $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ACB$: $AB =
AC$, $AC = AB$, $\angle A$ common. By SAS they are congruent, so $\angle B =
\angle C$. $\square$

**Converse.** If two angles of a triangle are equal, the triangle is
isosceles - the sides opposite the equal angles are equal. (Proof: the
same comparison with ASA.)

Consequences: an equilateral triangle has all angles equal; a
triangle with all angles equal is equilateral.

## Worked Example

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = AC$ and $\angle B = 65^\circ$. Find $\angle C$. Then:
in $\triangle MNK$, $\angle M = \angle N = 40^\circ$ and $MK = 6$ cm; find
$NK$. Then: the perimeter of an isosceles triangle is $30$ cm and its
base is $8$ cm; find the legs.

- **$AB = AC$:** base $BC$, base angles $\angle B$ and $\angle C$. By the
  theorem, $\angle C = \angle B = 65^\circ$.
- **$\angle M = \angle N$:** by the converse, the sides opposite them are
  equal: $NK$ (opposite $\angle M$) $= MK$ (opposite $\angle N$) $= 6$ cm.
- **Perimeter:** legs $x$ each, $2x + 8 = 30$, $x = 11$ cm.

An angle chase: $AB = AC$, and $\angle A = 40^\circ$. The base angles are
equal and (as the next topics show) all three sum to $180^\circ$, so
each base angle is $70^\circ$.

## Common Mistake

Picking the wrong angles as base angles. With $AB = AC$, the base is
$BC$ and the equal angles are at $B$ and $C$ - *not* at $A$. The equal
angles are the ones opposite the equal sides; the odd one out is at
the vertex where the equal sides meet. Write which side is the base
before assigning angles.

## The Takeaway

Equal sides face equal angles, and equal angles face equal sides - the
isosceles triangle theorem and its converse, both proved by comparing
the triangle with itself. In $\triangle ABC$ with $AB = AC$, the equal
angles are $\angle B$ and $\angle C$.

## Check Yourself

1. $AB = AC$ and $\angle C = 48^\circ$. Find $\angle B$.
2. $\angle P = \angle Q$ in $\triangle PQR$, $PR = 9$. Find $QR$.
3. An isosceles triangle has base $12$ and leg $10$. Find its perimeter.
4. Can an isosceles triangle have a base angle of $90^\circ$? Why not?
5. In $\triangle ABC$, $\angle A = \angle B = \angle C$. What kind of triangle
   is it?
6. Someone says in $\triangle ABC$ with $AB = AC$, "$\angle A = \angle B$."
   Which angles are actually equal?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $48^\circ$.
> 2. $9$.
> 3. $32$.
> 4. No - two base angles of $90^\circ$ already make $180^\circ$.
> 5. Equilateral.
> 6. $\angle B$ and $\angle C$ - the angles opposite the equal sides.
