## Why It Matters

Nothing stops you from taking more slices than one pizza has. Order two
pizzas cut into $8$, eat $11$ slices between you, and you've had
$\frac{11}{8}$ of a pizza - a perfectly good fraction, just bigger than
one whole. Fractions come in two sizes: less than a whole, and at least a
whole. Telling them apart at a glance is what lets you know whether an
answer is a piece of something or more than the thing itself.

## The Big Idea

Look at the numerator against the denominator. If the pieces you have
are fewer than the pieces in a whole - $3$ of $8$ - you have less than one
whole: a proper fraction. If you have at least as many - $8$ of $8$, or
$11$ of $8$ - you have a whole or more: an improper fraction. The name
"improper" doesn't mean wrong; it means the fraction is carrying more
than one whole and could be written as wholes plus a part.

On the number line, proper fractions all live between $0$ and $1$, and
improper ones sit at $1$ or to the right of it.

## The Rule

A fraction is **proper** if its numerator is *less than* its denominator:

$$
\frac{3}{8}, \quad \frac{1}{2}, \quad \frac{99}{100}
$$

Every proper fraction is less than $1$.

A fraction is **improper** if its numerator is *greater than or equal to*
its denominator:

$$
\frac{8}{8}, \quad \frac{11}{8}, \quad \frac{5}{2}
$$

Every improper fraction is greater than or equal to $1$. When the
numerator equals the denominator, the fraction equals $1$ exactly; when
the numerator is a multiple of the denominator, it's a natural number.

So to compare a fraction with $1$: proper means below, improper means at
or above. And every proper fraction is smaller than every improper one.

## Worked Example

Sort these into proper and improper: $\frac{7}{9}$, $\frac{9}{7}$,
$\frac{9}{9}$, $\frac{4}{13}$, $\frac{21}{7}$.

- $\frac{7}{9}$: $7 < 9$ - proper, less than $1$.
- $\frac{9}{7}$: $9 > 7$ - improper, more than $1$.
- $\frac{9}{9}$: equal - improper, and exactly $1$.
- $\frac{4}{13}$: $4 < 13$ - proper.
- $\frac{21}{7}$: $21 > 7$ - improper; and $21 : 7 = 3$, so it's the
  natural number $3$.

Now a fill-in: for which natural values of $n$ is $\frac{n}{6}$ proper?
Proper means $n < 6$, so $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. And $\frac{6}{n}$ is
improper when $6 \geq n$: $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$.

## Common Mistake

Thinking "improper" means the fraction is written wrongly and has to be
fixed. $\frac{11}{8}$ is a correct, complete number - it's eleven eighths,
and it sits at a definite point past $1$ on the number line. Later you'll
learn to rewrite it as a mixed number, and that's often handier, but it's
a change of clothes, not a correction.

## The Takeaway

Numerator smaller than denominator: proper, less than $1$. Numerator
equal or bigger: improper, one whole or more. The comparison with $1$ is
built into the name.

## Check Yourself

1. Which of $\frac{5}{6}$, $\frac{6}{5}$, $\frac{6}{6}$ are proper?
2. Write three improper fractions with denominator $4$.
3. Compare with $1$: $\frac{17}{18}$ and $\frac{18}{17}$.
4. For which natural $n$ is $\frac{n}{5}$ improper but less than $2$?
5. Which is bigger, $\frac{3}{4}$ or $\frac{5}{4}$? Do you need to look
   at the numerators?
6. Someone says $\frac{7}{7}$ is proper "because the numerator isn't
   bigger." What's the right classification?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Only $\frac{5}{6}$.
> 2. For example $\frac{4}{4}$, $\frac{7}{4}$, $\frac{13}{4}$.
> 3. $\frac{17}{18} < 1$ and $\frac{18}{17} > 1$.
> 4. $n = 5, 6, 7, 8, 9$ - at least $5$ to be improper, under $10$ to
>    stay below $2$.
> 5. $\frac{5}{4}$ - it's improper and $\frac{3}{4}$ is proper, so no
>    numerator check is needed.
> 6. Improper: equal counts as improper, and $\frac{7}{7} = 1$.
