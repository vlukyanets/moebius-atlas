## Why It Matters

"Row $7$, seat $12$." "B4 - hit!" "Go three blocks east and two north."
Every one of these pins down a place with two numbers. A map grid, a
chessboard, a spreadsheet, a screen full of pixels - all the same idea.
Give a flat surface two number lines at right angles, and every point
on it gets an address: a pair of numbers, and nothing more.

## The Big Idea

One number line locates a point on a line. To locate a point on a
*plane* you need two: how far along, and how far up. Lay two number
lines through the same origin, one horizontal and one vertical,
perpendicular to each other. From any point, drop a perpendicular to
each axis: where it lands on the horizontal axis is the first number,
where it lands on the vertical axis is the second. The pair $(3, -2)$
means "three to the right, two down." Order matters - $(3, -2)$ and
$(-2, 3)$ are different places.

## The Rule

A **coordinate plane** is a plane with two perpendicular number lines
through a common origin $O$: the horizontal **$x$-axis** (abscissa axis)
and the vertical **$y$-axis** (ordinate axis). The axes are the
**coordinate axes**, and they divide the plane into four **quadrants**,
numbered $I$ to $IV$ anticlockwise from the top right.

Each point $M$ has two **coordinates**, written $M(x; y)$: the
**abscissa** $x$ - the coordinate of the foot of the perpendicular from
$M$ to the $x$-axis - and the **ordinate** $y$ - likewise on the $y$-axis.
The first number is always $x$.

- The origin is $O(0; 0)$.
- Points on the $x$-axis have $y = 0$; points on the $y$-axis have $x = 0$.
- Quadrant $I$: $x > 0$, $y > 0$. Quadrant $II$: $x < 0$, $y > 0$. Quadrant
  $III$: both negative. Quadrant $IV$: $x > 0$, $y < 0$.

## Worked Example

Plot $A(3; -2)$, $B(-4; 1)$, $C(0; 3)$ and $D(-2; -3)$, and name the
quadrant of each.

- **$A(3; -2)$:** $3$ right along the $x$-axis, then $2$ down. Quadrant
  $IV$.
- **$B(-4; 1)$:** $4$ left, $1$ up. Quadrant $II$.
- **$C(0; 3)$:** no move sideways, $3$ up - it's on the $y$-axis, in no
  quadrant.
- **$D(-2; -3)$:** $2$ left, $3$ down. Quadrant $III$.

Reading coordinates: a point sits $5$ squares left of the $y$-axis and $4$
squares above the $x$-axis. Its coordinates are $(-5; 4)$ - the
horizontal number first.

Now a shape. Plot $(1; 1)$, $(5; 1)$, $(5; 4)$, $(1; 4)$ and join them in
order: a rectangle $4$ wide and $3$ tall.

## Common Mistake

Swapping the coordinates. $(3; -2)$ plotted as "three up, two left" lands
in the wrong quadrant entirely. The first number is *always* the
horizontal move, the second *always* the vertical one - "along the
corridor, then up the stairs." When in doubt, say which axis each
number belongs to before plotting.

## The Takeaway

Two perpendicular number lines give every point of the plane an
address $(x; y)$: $x$ along the horizontal axis, $y$ along the vertical,
in that order. The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, and the
signs of the coordinates say which one a point is in.

## Check Yourself

1. Which coordinate is written first, the abscissa or the ordinate?
2. In which quadrant is $(-6; 2)$? And $(4; -7)$?
3. Which axis does $(0; -5)$ lie on? And $(8; 0)$?
4. A point is $3$ squares right of the $y$-axis and $6$ squares below the
   $x$-axis. Write its coordinates.
5. Plot $(-2; 0)$, $(2; 0)$ and $(0; 3)$ and join them. What figure is
   it?
6. Someone plots $(2; -5)$ two units up and five units left. Where should
   the point be?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. The abscissa, $x$.
> 2. $II$; $IV$.
> 3. The $y$-axis; the $x$-axis.
> 4. $(3; -6)$.
> 5. A triangle (isosceles).
> 6. Two units right, five units down - quadrant $IV$.
