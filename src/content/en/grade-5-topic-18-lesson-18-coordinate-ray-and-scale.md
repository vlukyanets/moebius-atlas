## Why It Matters

A ruler, a thermometer, the speedometer in a car, the volume bar on a
phone - all of them turn a number into a place on a line. Read the mark,
and you know the value; move along the line, and the value changes. Once
numbers have places, comparing them is just looking at which is further
along - and that picture, the number line, will follow you through every
maths course there is.

## The Big Idea

Take a ray. Put $0$ at its origin. Choose a step - one centimetre, one
square of the grid, anything - and walk along the ray, marking $1$ after
the first step, $2$ after the second, $3$ after the third, and on forever.
The ray never ends, so the numbers never run out. Now every natural number
has an address on the ray, and every marked point has a number. That's a
coordinate ray, and the number attached to a point is its coordinate.

A scale is the same idea wrapped around a real instrument. The marks on a
thermometer or a measuring jug are a coordinate ray with a unit chosen to
suit the job - and the only skill is reading what one small division is
worth.

## The Rule

A **coordinate ray** is a ray with three things fixed on it: the
**origin** $O$, which is given the number $0$; a **direction**, marked by
an arrow; and a **unit segment**, the distance from $0$ to $1$. Every
step of one unit segment moves to the next natural number.

The number matched to a point is its **coordinate**, written in brackets:
$A(3)$ means the point $A$ has coordinate $3$, three unit segments from
$O$.

On a coordinate ray, **the point further from the origin has the bigger
coordinate** - so of two numbers, the one further right is greater. The
distance between two points is the difference of their coordinates: from
$A(3)$ to $B(7)$ is $7 - 3 = 4$ units.

A **scale** is a line of marks with numbers on some of them. The **value
of a division** is what one gap between neighbouring marks is worth. To
find it, take two labelled marks, subtract, and divide by the number of
gaps between them.

## Worked Example

A thermometer has labelled marks at $20$ and $30$, with four unlabelled
marks between them. The liquid stands at the third unlabelled mark above
$20$. What's the temperature?

- **Count the gaps.** Between $20$ and $30$ there are four unlabelled
  marks, so five gaps.
- **Value of a division:** $(30 - 20) : 5 = 2$ degrees per gap.
- **Read off:** three gaps above $20$ is $20 + 3 \cdot 2 = 26$.

The temperature is $26$ degrees.

Now a coordinate ray with a unit segment of one grid square. Mark $A(2)$,
$B(5)$ and $C(9)$. $B$ is further along than $A$, so $5 > 2$. The distance
$AC$ is $9 - 2 = 7$ units.

## Common Mistake

Counting marks instead of gaps. Between $20$ and $30$ there are four
marks, and someone divides $10$ by $4$ - but the four marks make five
gaps, and it's the gaps that carry the value. Draw a finger along the
scale and count the jumps, not the lines.

## The Takeaway

A coordinate ray gives every natural number a place, in order, one unit
segment apart - the further from $0$, the bigger. A scale is a coordinate
ray on an instrument, and reading it starts with finding what one
division is worth.

## Check Yourself

1. On a coordinate ray with unit segment $1$ cm, how far from the origin
   is the point $M(6)$?
2. Which point is further from the origin, $A(14)$ or $B(9)$? Which
   coordinate is bigger?
3. Find the distance between $P(4)$ and $Q(15)$.
4. A scale has labels $0$ and $50$ with nine marks between them. What's
   the value of a division?
5. A measuring jug shows $100$ ml and $200$ ml with three marks between.
   The water stands at the second mark above $100$. How much water is
   there?
6. Someone says the distance between $C(3)$ and $D(10)$ is $8$, "because
   there are eight numbers from $3$ to $10$." What's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $6$ cm.
> 2. $A$; $14 > 9$.
> 3. $15 - 4 = 11$.
> 4. Nine marks make ten gaps: $50 : 10 = 5$.
> 5. Four gaps of $25$ ml each; two gaps above $100$ is $150$ ml.
> 6. $10 - 3 = 7$ - the distance is the difference of the coordinates,
>    which counts the steps, not the numbers.
