## Why It Matters

You've been solving equations for two years. Now it's time to ask the
questions a mathematician asks: what exactly *is* an equation, when
have you found *all* its roots, and when is it fair to replace one
equation by another? Two equations that look different can have the
same roots - and knowing when that's guaranteed is what lets you
transform an equation step by step without ever losing or inventing a
solution.

## The Big Idea

An equation is a question: "for which values of the letter are the two
sides equal?" The answer is the set of roots - maybe one number, maybe
several, maybe none, maybe every number there is. $x + 3 = 3 + x$ is true
for any $x$; $x + 1 = x + 2$ is true for none; $x^2 = 4$ has two roots.
"Solve" means "find the whole answer," not "find a number that works."

When you transform an equation - move a term, divide both sides - you
replace it by a new one. That's only safe if the new equation has
exactly the same roots as the old: no root lost, none gained. Such
equations are called equivalent, and the two moves from grade 6 always
produce equivalent equations. Other moves, like multiplying both sides
by an expression that could be zero, might not.

## The Rule

An **equation in one variable** is an equality containing a variable.
A **root** (solution) is a value of the variable that turns it into a
true numerical equality. **To solve** an equation is to find all its
roots or to prove there are none.

An equation can have one root, several roots, no roots, or infinitely
many.

Two equations are **equivalent** if they have the same roots (or both
have none). Equivalent transformations:

1. moving a term to the other side with its sign changed - that is,
   adding the same expression to both sides;
2. multiplying or dividing both sides by the same **non-zero** number.

Expanding brackets and collecting like terms on either side also give
an equivalent equation, since they don't change the value of a side.

## Worked Example

Decide whether $2$ is a root of $3x - 1 = x + 3$; then find all the roots
of $2(x + 1) = 2x + 2$ and of $x - 5 = x + 1$.

- **Is $2$ a root?** Substitute: left $3 \cdot 2 - 1 = 5$, right $2 + 3 =
  5$. Equal - yes, $2$ is a root. (Whether it's the *only* root needs
  solving: $2x = 4$, $x = 2$. It is.)
- **$2(x + 1) = 2x + 2$.** Expand: $2x + 2 = 2x + 2$. Any $x$ makes this
  true - the equation has infinitely many roots: every number.
- **$x - 5 = x + 1$.** Move $x$ across: $x - x = 1 + 5$, so $0 = 6$. False
  for every $x$ - no roots.

Are $x + 2 = 5$ and $2x = 6$ equivalent? Both have the single root $3$ -
yes. Are $x = 3$ and $x^2 = 9$ equivalent? No: the second also has the
root $-3$.

## Common Mistake

Stopping at one root. "$x^2 = 9$, and $3$ works, so $x = 3$" misses
$-3$. Solving means finding *all* the roots. And the reverse error:
declaring "no roots" because the first number you tried failed. Test
values check a candidate; only the equivalent transformations prove
what the whole set of roots is.

## The Takeaway

An equation asks for every value that makes the two sides equal;
solving it means the whole set - one, many, none, or all. Equivalent
equations share their roots, and the two safe moves - transposing
terms and multiplying by a non-zero number - always keep equations
equivalent.

## Check Yourself

1. Is $-1$ a root of $x^2 + 3x = -2$?
2. How many roots does $3(x - 1) = 3x - 3$ have?
3. How many roots does $4x + 7 = 4x$ have?
4. Are $x - 2 = 0$ and $3x = 6$ equivalent? Why?
5. Are $x = 2$ and $x(x - 2) = 0$ equivalent? Why not?
6. Someone solves $x^2 = 16$, tries $4$, and writes "$x = 4$." What's
   missing?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Yes: $1 - 3 = -2$.
> 2. Infinitely many - both sides are the same expression.
> 3. None - $7 = 0$ is false.
> 4. Yes - both have the single root $2$.
> 5. No - the second also has the root $0$.
> 6. The root $-4$; solving means all roots.
