## Why It Matters

"For which $x$ is $2x - 3 > 5$?" is a different kind of question from
"for which $x$ is $2x - 3 = 5$". The equation has one answer; the
inequality has infinitely many - every number past $4$. Before solving
such things you need the vocabulary: what a solution is, what it
means to solve, and which rearrangements are allowed. It's the
equations chapter of grade 7 over again, with one twist at the
negative sign.

## The Big Idea

An inequality with a variable is a question with a yes/no answer for
each value of the variable. Plug in $x = 5$ into $2x - 3 > 5$: $7 > 5$,
yes - $5$ is a solution. Plug in $x = 1$: $-1 > 5$, no. The set of all
the yeses is what "solving" means to find, and usually it's a whole
stretch of the number line.

To find it you transform the inequality into simpler ones with the
same solutions - **equivalent** ones - using exactly the properties
of numerical inequalities: move terms across, multiply by positives
freely, multiply by negatives with a flip.

## The Rule

A **solution** of an inequality with one variable is a value of the
variable that turns it into a true numerical inequality. To **solve**
an inequality is to find all its solutions or to show there are none.

Two inequalities are **equivalent** if they have the same solutions.

**Equivalent transformations:**

1. a term may be moved from one side to the other with its sign
   changed;
2. both sides may be multiplied or divided by the same **positive**
   number;
3. both sides may be multiplied or divided by the same **negative**
   number, **reversing** the sign of the inequality.

An inequality that holds for every value of the variable (like $x^2 +
1 > 0$) is proved, not solved; one with no solutions (like $x^2 < 0$)
is said to have none.

## Worked Example

Is $x = 3$ a solution of $x^2 - 2x < 5$? Is $x = -2$? Then decide whether
$3x - 1 > 2x + 4$ and $x > 5$ are equivalent, and whether $-x < 2$ and $x <
-2$ are.

- **$x = 3$:** $9 - 6 = 3 < 5$ - yes. **$x = -2$:** $4 + 4 = 8 < 5$ - no.
- **$3x - 1 > 2x + 4$:** move $2x$ left and $-1$ right (rule 1): $x > 5$.
  Yes, equivalent.
- **$-x < 2$:** multiply by $-1$ and flip (rule 3): $x > -2$. So $-x < 2$
  is equivalent to $x > -2$, **not** to $x < -2$. Check: $x = 0$ satisfies
  $-0 < 2$ and $0 > -2$, but not $0 < -2$.

Prove $(x - 1)^2 + 3 > 0$ for all $x$: a square is $\geq 0$, plus $3$ is at
least $3$. Every real number is a solution.

## Common Mistake

Treating a solution as a single number. "Solve $x + 2 > 5$" - answer
"$x = 4$". Yes, $4$ is *a* solution, but so are $3.5$, $100$ and $\pi$.
The answer is all $x > 3$, and stopping at one number is stopping
short. The reverse slip: writing $x > 3$ when the question asked
whether a *specific* number is a solution - then the answer is just
yes or no, obtained by substituting.

## The Takeaway

A solution is a value that makes the inequality true; solving means
finding all of them, usually a stretch of the line. Rearrange with
the three equivalent transformations, flipping the sign whenever you
multiply or divide by a negative.

## Check Yourself

1. Is $x = 2$ a solution of $3x - 1 \geq 5$? Is $x = 1$?
2. Which of $-1, 0, 1, 2$ are solutions of $x^2 < 2$?
3. Are $2x > 6$ and $x > 3$ equivalent? Why?
4. Are $-3x > 6$ and $x > -2$ equivalent?
5. Prove that $x^2 + 4x + 5 > 0$ for all $x$.
6. Someone says "$x < 4$ has the solution $x = 3$". What's incomplete?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Yes ($5 \geq 5$); no ($2 \geq 5$ is false).
> 2. $-1, 0, 1$.
> 3. Yes - divided by $2 > 0$.
> 4. No - dividing by $-3$ gives $x < -2$.
> 5. $x^2 + 4x + 5 = (x + 2)^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$.
> 6. $3$ is one solution among infinitely many; the solutions are all
>    numbers less than $4$.
