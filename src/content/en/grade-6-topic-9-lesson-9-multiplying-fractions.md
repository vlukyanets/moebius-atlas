## Why It Matters

Half of a half is a quarter - everyone knows that. But what's
$\frac{2}{3}$ of $\frac{3}{4}$ of a cake? Or the area of a rectangle
$\frac{5}{6}$ m by $\frac{3}{10}$ m? "Of" is a multiplication, and once
fractions can be multiplied, every recipe scaled, every area with
fractional sides and every "part of a part" question becomes one short
line.

## The Big Idea

Take a rectangle one metre by one metre - area $1$. Cut it into thirds
one way and quarters the other: twelve little cells, each $\frac{1}{12}$
of the square. A piece $\frac{2}{3}$ wide and $\frac{3}{4}$ tall covers
$2$ columns by $3$ rows of cells - $6$ cells, so $\frac{6}{12}$, which is
$\frac{1}{2}$. Numerators multiplied ($2 \cdot 3$) count the cells inside;
denominators multiplied ($3 \cdot 4$) count the cells in the whole
square. That's why multiplying fractions is "top times top, bottom
times bottom."

## The Rule

To multiply two fractions, multiply the numerators and multiply the
denominators:

$$
\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d}
$$

Cancel *before* multiplying whenever a numerator and a denominator share
a factor - it keeps the numbers small.

A natural number is a fraction over $1$: $\frac{2}{7} \cdot 5 = \frac{2
\cdot 5}{7} = \frac{10}{7}$. Mixed numbers are turned into improper
fractions first.

Multiplying by a fraction less than $1$ makes a number smaller;
multiplying by a fraction greater than $1$ makes it bigger. The
commutative, associative and distributive properties hold as always.

## Worked Example

Find $\frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{9}{10}$.

- **Look for cancelling:** $5$ (top) and $10$ (bottom) share $5$; $9$ (top)
  and $6$ (bottom) share $3$.
- **Cancel:** $\frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{9}{10} = \frac{1 \cdot 3}{2 \cdot 2}$.
- **Multiply what's left:** $\frac{3}{4}$.

Without cancelling first you'd get $\frac{45}{60}$ and then cancel by $15$
to the same $\frac{3}{4}$.

Now mixed numbers: $2\frac{1}{4} \cdot 1\frac{1}{3}$. Convert: $\frac{9}{4}
\cdot \frac{4}{3}$. Cancel the $4$s and $9$ with $3$: $\frac{3}{1} \cdot
\frac{1}{1} = 3$.

## Common Mistake

Bringing the fractions to a common denominator. $\frac{1}{2} \cdot
\frac{1}{3}$ is not $\frac{3}{6} \cdot \frac{2}{6} = \frac{6}{36}$ - well,
it is, but only by accident after cancelling, and it's pointless work.
Common denominators are for adding. Multiplication takes the fractions
as they are: top times top, bottom times bottom.

The other trap: multiplying mixed numbers part by part. $2\frac{1}{4}
\cdot 1\frac{1}{3}$ is not $2 \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{3}$.
Convert to improper fractions first.

## The Takeaway

Top times top over bottom times bottom - and cancel across before you
multiply. Mixed numbers become improper fractions first; a natural
number sits over $1$.

## Check Yourself

1. Find $\frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{7}$.
2. Find $\frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{3}{8}$, cancelling first.
3. Find $\frac{7}{12} \cdot 6$.
4. Find $1\frac{2}{5} \cdot 2\frac{1}{7}$.
5. A rectangle is $\frac{5}{6}$ m by $\frac{3}{10}$ m. Find its area.
6. Someone computes $2\frac{1}{2} \cdot 3\frac{1}{3}$ as $6\frac{1}{6}$.
   Where's the mistake, and what's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $\frac{6}{35}$.
> 2. $\frac{1 \cdot 1}{3 \cdot 2} = \frac{1}{6}$.
> 3. $\frac{7}{2} = 3\frac{1}{2}$.
> 4. $\frac{7}{5} \cdot \frac{15}{7} = 3$.
> 5. $\frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{1}{4}\ \text{m}^2$.
> 6. Parts were multiplied separately; $\frac{5}{2} \cdot \frac{10}{3} =
>    \frac{25}{3} = 8\frac{1}{3}$.
