## Why It Matters

Which is more, $\frac{5}{8}$ of a pizza or $\frac{2}{3}$? The slices are
different sizes, so counting them tells you nothing. In grade 5 you
could only compare fractions cut the same way. Now you can re-cut them:
bring both to the same denominator, and the old rule - more pieces
wins - works again. This is also the door to adding fractions with
different denominators, which is next.

## The Big Idea

Eighths and thirds don't match, but there's a slice size that both can
be cut into: twenty-fourths. An eighth is three twenty-fourths, a third
is eight twenty-fourths. Re-cut both pizzas that finely and you can
compare pieces directly: $\frac{5}{8}$ is $15$ pieces, $\frac{2}{3}$ is
$16$ pieces. So $\frac{2}{3}$ is more.

Which slice size works? Any common multiple of the denominators - and the
smallest one, the least common multiple, gives the least work. That's
why the LCM from grade 5 comes back here under a new name: the least
common denominator.

## The Rule

The **least common denominator** of several fractions is the least
common multiple of their denominators.

**To bring fractions to a common denominator:**

1. find the LCM of the denominators - that's the new denominator;
2. for each fraction, divide the new denominator by the old one - that's
   its additional factor;
3. multiply each numerator by its additional factor.

**To compare fractions with different denominators**, bring them to a
common denominator and compare the numerators. Two quick shortcuts:
of two fractions with the same numerator, the one with the *smaller*
denominator is bigger ($\frac{2}{3} > \frac{2}{5}$); and a fraction is
compared with $\frac{1}{2}$ or with $1$ by looking at whether its
numerator is more or less than half, or all, of its denominator.

## Worked Example

Compare $\frac{5}{8}$ and $\frac{7}{12}$.

- **LCM of $8$ and $12$:** $8 = 2^3$, $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, so LCM $= 2^3
  \cdot 3 = 24$.
- **Additional factors:** $24 : 8 = 3$ for the first, $24 : 12 = 2$ for
  the second.
- **Rewrite:** $\frac{5}{8} = \frac{15}{24}$, $\frac{7}{12} = \frac{14}{24}$.
- **Compare numerators:** $15 > 14$, so $\frac{5}{8} > \frac{7}{12}$.

Now three fractions at once: $\frac{1}{6}$, $\frac{3}{4}$, $\frac{5}{9}$.
LCM of $6$, $4$, $9$ is $36$. Factors $6$, $9$, $4$: $\frac{6}{36}$,
$\frac{27}{36}$, $\frac{20}{36}$. In order: $\frac{1}{6} < \frac{5}{9} <
\frac{3}{4}$.

## Common Mistake

Changing the denominator and forgetting the numerator. $\frac{5}{8}$
rewritten as $\frac{5}{24}$ is a different, much smaller number - the
pizza was re-cut into finer slices but the count of slices wasn't
updated. The additional factor multiplies *both*: it's the basic
property of a fraction, and it never touches one number alone.

## The Takeaway

To compare - or later add - fractions cut differently, re-cut them the
same way: the least common denominator is the LCM of the denominators,
and each numerator is multiplied by the same factor as its denominator
was. Then the numerators decide.

## Check Yourself

1. Find the least common denominator of $\frac{3}{10}$ and $\frac{4}{15}$.
2. Bring $\frac{2}{9}$ and $\frac{5}{6}$ to a common denominator.
3. Compare $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{5}{7}$.
4. Compare $\frac{4}{9}$ and $\frac{4}{11}$ without a common denominator.
5. Which is bigger than $\frac{1}{2}$: $\frac{7}{15}$ or $\frac{9}{16}$?
6. Someone rewrites $\frac{2}{3}$ with denominator $12$ as $\frac{2}{12}$.
   What's the correct fraction?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $30$.
> 2. $\frac{4}{18}$ and $\frac{15}{18}$.
> 3. $\frac{21}{28} > \frac{20}{28}$, so $\frac{3}{4} > \frac{5}{7}$.
> 4. $\frac{4}{9} > \frac{4}{11}$ - same numerator, smaller denominator.
> 5. $\frac{9}{16}$ - nine is more than half of sixteen; seven is less
>    than half of fifteen.
> 6. $\frac{8}{12}$ - the numerator is multiplied by $4$ too.
