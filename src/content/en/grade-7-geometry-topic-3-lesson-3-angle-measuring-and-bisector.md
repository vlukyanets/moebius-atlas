## Why It Matters

Angles are the second thing geometry measures, and the properties of
their measure are the twins of the properties for segments: every
angle has a degree measure, and a ray inside an angle splits its
measure into two parts that add up. Almost every theorem about
triangles is really a statement about adding and comparing angle
measures - so these two facts, and the ray that halves an angle, are
the tools you'll reach for constantly.

## The Big Idea

You measured angles with a protractor in grade 5. What makes that
measuring trustworthy is the same pair of properties as for segments.
Each angle has one definite measure, between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$,
with the straight angle at the top of the scale. And if a ray starts at
the vertex and runs inside the angle, it cuts the angle into two
angles whose measures add to the whole. Everything else - comparing
angles, finding an unknown angle from known ones - is arithmetic on
those measures.

One ray gets a name: the one that cuts the angle into two *equal*
parts, the bisector. Every angle has exactly one.

## The Rule

An **angle** is a figure formed by two rays with a common origin - its
**sides** and **vertex**. An angle whose sides are complementary rays
is a **straight angle**.

**Basic properties of measuring angles:**

- Every angle has a definite **degree measure**, a positive number; a
  straight angle measures $180^\circ$.
- If a ray from the vertex passes between the sides of an angle, the
  measure of the angle equals the sum of the measures of the two
  angles it is divided into.

Angles are **acute** ($< 90^\circ$), **right** ($= 90^\circ$), or
**obtuse** (between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$). Two angles are **equal**
if their measures are equal.

The **bisector** of an angle is the ray from the vertex that divides
the angle into two equal angles. Each angle has exactly one bisector.

## Worked Example

The ray $OC$ lies inside $\angle AOB = 120^\circ$, and $\angle AOC$ is
$20^\circ$ more than $\angle COB$. Find both. Then: $OK$ is the bisector of
$\angle MON$, and $\angle MOK = 35^\circ$; find $\angle MON$.

- **Let $\angle COB = x$.** Then $\angle AOC = x + 20$.
- **Add up:** $x + (x + 20) = 120$, so $2x = 100$, $x = 50$.
- **Answer:** $\angle COB = 50^\circ$, $\angle AOC = 70^\circ$. Check: $70 +
  50 = 120$.
- **Bisector:** $\angle MOK = \angle KON = 35^\circ$, so $\angle MON = 70^\circ$.

One more: $OD$ is the bisector of $\angle AOB$, and $\angle AOD = 48^\circ$.
Is $\angle AOB$ acute or obtuse? $\angle AOB = 96^\circ$ - obtuse.

## Common Mistake

Using the additive property for a ray that doesn't lie inside the
angle. If $OC$ is *outside* $\angle AOB$, then $\angle AOC$ and $\angle COB$
don't add up to $\angle AOB$ - one of them contains the other. Check
where the ray runs before adding; the property has a condition, and
a wrong picture breaks it silently.

## The Takeaway

Angle measures are positive numbers up to $180^\circ$, and a ray inside
an angle splits the measure into two parts that add to the whole. The
bisector is the ray that makes the two parts equal - one per angle.

## Check Yourself

1. $OC$ is inside $\angle AOB$; $\angle AOC = 34^\circ$, $\angle COB = 71^\circ$.
   Find $\angle AOB$ and name its kind.
2. $\angle AOB = 150^\circ$ and $OC$ inside it makes $\angle AOC = 90^\circ$.
   Find $\angle COB$.
3. $OK$ bisects $\angle AOB = 84^\circ$. Find $\angle AOK$.
4. $OK$ bisects $\angle AOB$, and $\angle KOB = 63^\circ$. Find $\angle AOB$.
5. Can the bisector of an obtuse angle make an obtuse angle with a
   side? Why not?
6. Someone adds $\angle AOC$ and $\angle COB$ to get $\angle AOB$ although
   $OC$ lies outside $\angle AOB$. What's the mistake?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $105^\circ$, obtuse.
> 2. $60^\circ$.
> 3. $42^\circ$.
> 4. $126^\circ$.
> 5. No - each half is less than $90^\circ$, since the whole is under
>    $180^\circ$.
> 6. The additive property needs the ray inside the angle; outside, one
>    angle is the *difference* of the others.
