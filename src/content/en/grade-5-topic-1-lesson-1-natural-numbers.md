## Why It Matters

Count the money in your wallet, check how many likes a post got, or read out
a phone number - every single time, you're using natural numbers. They're
the oldest mathematical tool humans ever built, invented thousands of years
before anyone wrote down a single symbol, just so a shepherd could tell if a
sheep had gone missing. Everything else in math - fractions, negative
numbers, even algebra - grew out of this one simple idea: how many?

## The Big Idea

Picture a row of glass jars lined up left to right: a ones-jar, a tens-jar, a
hundreds-jar, a thousands-jar, and so on - each new jar ten times bigger
than the one before it. You can drop up to nine marbles into any jar. The
moment a tenth marble would go in, that's not allowed: instead, all ten
marbles get bundled up and moved one jar to the left, where they count as a
single marble. "Ten small ones become one big one" - that's the whole idea
behind writing numbers with digits. Read a written number
from right to left, and you're reading straight off those jars: ones, then
tens, then hundreds. And every three jars in a row make up one shelf - the
ones-shelf, then the thousands-shelf, then the millions-shelf - which is
exactly why huge numbers get split into groups of three when they're written
down.

## The Rule

The natural numbers are the numbers you count with:

$$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, \dots$$

They never stop - there's no last one, no matter how far you go.

Every natural number is a string of digits, each one from $0$ to $9$. The
digit $0$ is a bit special: by itself, it writes the number **zero**, which
means "none" - a football score of 2:0 says the second team scored no goals
at all. Counting never starts at "none," so **zero is not a natural
number.** But it still shows up in numbers, in a place that would otherwise
be empty: in $305$, that $0$ means there are no tens.

To keep long numbers readable, digits are grouped in threes from the right -
ones, then thousands, then millions, then billions - each group called a
**class**. A million is a thousand thousands, $1\,000\,000$; a billion is a
thousand millions, $1\,000\,000\,000$. Grouped this way, $37\,142\,015$ reads
as "$37$ million, $142$ thousand, $15$" - three short pieces instead of one
long string of digits.

There's another way to take a number apart: as the sum of what each of its
digits is worth.

$$7\,213\,049 = 7\,000\,000 + 200\,000 + 10\,000 + 3000 + 40 + 9$$

Each term is a digit times its place value - $7$ millions, $2$
hundred-thousands, $1$ ten-thousand, $3$ thousands, $4$ tens, $9$ ones - and
adding them all back up gives the number you started with.

## Worked Example

Write the number "thirteen million, two thousand" using digits.

- **Split the phrase into classes.** "Thirteen million" is the millions
  class. "Two thousand" is the thousands class. Nothing is said about the
  ones class - so that means zero.
- **Fill in the millions class.** Thirteen is just $13$. It's the leftmost
  class, so it doesn't need any leading zeros.
- **Fill in the thousands class.** It only has a $2$ - but a class always
  has three spots (hundreds, tens, ones of that class), not one. So you
  write $002$: once a class isn't the leftmost one, its empty spots must be
  filled with zeros.
- **Fill in the ones class.** Nothing was said about it at all - so it's all
  zeros too: $000$.

Put the classes together: $13\,002\,000$.

## Common Mistake

The easiest mistake is forgetting the zeros where a class is missing digits.
Write "thirteen million, two thousand" as $13\,2\,000$ or as $132\,000$, and
you get a completely different number - "one hundred thirty-two thousand,"
not "thirteen million, two thousand." Every class always has exactly three
digits, except the very first one on the left - and its empty spots must
always be filled with zeros.

## The Takeaway

Natural numbers are the numbers you count with - and however far you count,
there's always one more. Zero isn't one of them: it's just the digit that
marks an empty place.

## Check Yourself

1. Write "seven million three hundred thousand forty" using digits.
2. Read $408\,015$ out loud. How many classes does it have?
3. Break $52\,706$ into place-value terms.
4. Which digit of $3049$ stands in the tens place? And in the thousands
   place?
5. Is zero a natural number? Say why in one sentence.
6. Someone wrote "twenty million five thousand" as $20\,5000$. What went
   wrong, and what is the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $7\,300\,040$.
> 2. "$408$ thousand, $15$" - two classes, thousands and ones.
> 3. $50\,000 + 2000 + 700 + 6$.
> 4. $4$ in the tens place, $3$ in the thousands place.
> 5. No - counting starts at one, and zero means "none."
> 6. The empty spots of the thousands class weren't filled with zeros; it
>    should be $20\,005\,000$.
