## Why It Matters

Every polygon can be cut into triangles, every roof truss and bridge
is made of them, and every theorem about shapes in this course goes
through them. The triangle is geometry's basic unit. Before proving
anything about it, its parts need names and its kinds need sorting -
and one useful vocabulary word arrives: what it means for two
triangles to be the same.

## The Big Idea

Three points not on a line, joined in pairs: three sides, three
angles, three vertices. Each angle sits at a vertex, opposite the
side that doesn't touch that vertex - so "the angle opposite side $AB$"
is $\angle C$. Triangles sort two ways, exactly as in grade 5: by sides
(scalene, isosceles, equilateral) and by angles (acute, right,
obtuse), and every triangle has one label from each list.

Two triangles are equal when they can be matched up vertex to vertex
so that all three sides and all three angles agree. Saying $\triangle
ABC = \triangle A_1B_1C_1$ isn't just "equal"; it says *which* vertex
matches which - $A$ with $A_1$, and so on - and that ordering carries
all six equalities.

## The Rule

A **triangle** $ABC$ is a figure made of three points $A$, $B$, $C$ not
on one line - its **vertices** - and the three segments $AB$, $BC$, $CA$
joining them - its **sides**. Its **angles** are $\angle A = \angle BAC$,
$\angle B$, $\angle C$. The side $AB$ lies **opposite** the vertex $C$ and
the angle $\angle C$.

The **perimeter** is $P = AB + BC + CA$.

By sides: **scalene**, **isosceles** (two equal sides, the third the
**base**), **equilateral**. By angles: **acute**, **right**, **obtuse**.

Two triangles $ABC$ and $A_1B_1C_1$ are **equal** (congruent) if their
corresponding sides and angles are equal:

$$
AB = A_1B_1,\ BC = B_1C_1,\ CA = C_1A_1,\quad \angle A = \angle A_1,\ \angle B = \angle B_1,\ \angle C = \angle C_1
$$

The notation $\triangle ABC = \triangle A_1B_1C_1$ lists corresponding
vertices in the same order.

## Worked Example

In $\triangle ABC$, name the side opposite $\angle B$, the angle opposite
$AC$, and the two angles adjacent to side $BC$. Then: $\triangle ABC =
\triangle KLM$, $AB = 5$, $BC = 7$, $\angle C = 40^\circ$ - find $KL$, $LM$,
$\angle M$.

- **Opposite $\angle B$:** the side not touching $B$ - $AC$.
- **Opposite $AC$:** $\angle B$.
- **Adjacent to $BC$:** the angles at its ends, $\angle B$ and $\angle C$.
- **Congruence:** $A \leftrightarrow K$, $B \leftrightarrow L$, $C
  \leftrightarrow M$. So $KL = AB = 5$, $LM = BC = 7$, $\angle M = \angle C =
  40^\circ$.

Perimeter: an isosceles triangle with base $6$ and perimeter $20$ has
equal sides $(20 - 6) : 2 = 7$ each.

## Common Mistake

Reading $\triangle ABC = \triangle KLM$ without respecting the order.
$KL$ corresponds to $AB$ because $K$, $L$ are the first two letters and
so are $A$, $B$ - not because of any picture. Match letters by
position; then $\angle M$ is the angle at the third vertex, matching
$\angle C$, whatever the sketch suggests.

## The Takeaway

A triangle has three vertices, sides and angles; each side faces the
vertex and angle it doesn't touch. Two triangles are equal when the
six corresponding parts are equal, and the order of the letters in
$\triangle ABC = \triangle A_1B_1C_1$ is what says which part
corresponds to which.

## Check Yourself

1. In $\triangle PQR$, which angle is opposite $PR$? Which side is
   opposite $\angle P$?
2. $\triangle ABC = \triangle DEF$, $\angle A = 50^\circ$, $EF = 8$. Find
   $\angle D$ and $BC$.
3. An isosceles triangle has base $10$ and side $13$. Find its perimeter.
4. Can a triangle be both right and isosceles? Both obtuse and
   equilateral?
5. $\triangle ABC = \triangle BCA$ - what does that say about the sides?
6. Someone reads $\triangle ABC = \triangle KLM$ as "$BC = KL$." What's
   the correct correspondence?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $\angle Q$; $QR$.
> 2. $50^\circ$; $8$.
> 3. $36$.
> 4. Yes; no.
> 5. $AB = BC = CA$ - equilateral.
> 6. $BC$ corresponds to $LM$; $KL$ corresponds to $AB$.
