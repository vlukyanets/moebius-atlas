## Why It Matters

How much water fills the aquarium? How many boxes fit in the van? How
much air is in the room, how much concrete in the foundation, how much
does the fridge hold? Surface area was about the outside; these are
about the *inside* - the space a solid takes up. That's volume, and for
a box it's one multiplication, plus a little care with units.

## The Big Idea

Length was measured in unit segments, area in unit squares, and volume
is measured in unit cubes - a cube one centimetre on every edge, say.
Fill a box $5$ cm long, $3$ cm wide and $2$ cm high with such cubes: the
bottom layer is $5$ rows of $3$, that's $15$ cubes, and there are $2$
layers - $30$ cubes. Length times width gives the layer, times height
gives the stack: $V = abc$.

Units of volume are cubes of units of length, and they grow fast. A
decimetre is $10$ cm, so a cubic decimetre is $10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 =
1000$ cubic centimetres - and a cubic decimetre is exactly one litre.

## The Rule

The **volume** of a solid is the number of unit cubes that fill it.
Units: $\text{mm}^3$, $\text{cm}^3$, $\text{dm}^3$, $\text{m}^3$, $\text{km}^3$.

Volume of a cuboid with dimensions $a$, $b$, $c$:

$$
V = a \cdot b \cdot c = S_{\text{base}} \cdot c
$$

- the area of the base times the height. Volume of a cube with edge $a$:

$$
V = a^3
$$

That's why $a^3$ is called "$a$ cubed."

Converting: each step up in length is $10$, so each step in volume is
$10^3 = 1000$:

$$
1\ \text{cm}^3 = 1000\ \text{mm}^3, \qquad 1\ \text{dm}^3 = 1000\ \text{cm}^3, \qquad 1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000\ \text{dm}^3
$$

And **$1$ litre $= 1\ \text{dm}^3$**, so $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000$ litres and
$1$ ml $= 1\ \text{cm}^3$.

## Worked Example

An aquarium is $60$ cm long, $30$ cm wide and $40$ cm high. How many
litres of water does it hold when full?

- **Volume in $\text{cm}^3$:** $V = 60 \cdot 30 \cdot 40 = 72\,000\
  \text{cm}^3$.
- **To litres:** $1$ litre is $1000\ \text{cm}^3$, so $72\,000 : 1000 = 72$
  litres.

Backwards: a cuboid tank holds $2.4\ \text{m}^3$ and its base is $2$ m by
$1.5$ m. How deep is it? $V = S_{\text{base}} \cdot c$, so $c = 2.4 : (2
\cdot 1.5) = 2.4 : 3 = 0.8$ m.

A cube of edge $3$ dm: $V = 27\ \text{dm}^3 = 27$ litres. And a room $5$ m
by $4$ m by $2.5$ m holds $50\ \text{m}^3$ of air.

## Common Mistake

Converting cubic units like linear ones. $1\ \text{m}^3$ is not $100\
\text{cm}^3$ - a metre cube is $100$ cm along each of three edges, so
$100 \cdot 100 \cdot 100 = 1\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$. Every step of $10$ in
length is a step of $1000$ in volume. Picture the cube of little cubes
before you convert.

## The Takeaway

Volume counts unit cubes: for a cuboid, length times width times
height - base area times height; for a cube, the edge cubed. Cubic
units go up by $1000$ at each step, and a litre is a cubic decimetre.

## Check Yourself

1. Find the volume of a cuboid $8$ cm by $5$ cm by $4$ cm.
2. Find the volume of a cube with edge $6$ dm, in litres.
3. Express $3.5\ \text{m}^3$ in $\text{dm}^3$, and $4500\ \text{cm}^3$ in
   litres.
4. A box has base $12\ \text{cm}^2$ and volume $84\ \text{cm}^3$. Find its
   height.
5. A pool is $25$ m by $10$ m by $2$ m. How many litres does it hold?
6. Someone converts $2\ \text{m}^3$ to $200\ \text{cm}^3$. What's the right
   answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $160\ \text{cm}^3$.
> 2. $216\ \text{dm}^3 = 216$ litres.
> 3. $3500\ \text{dm}^3$; $4.5$ litres.
> 4. $84 : 12 = 7$ cm.
> 5. $500\ \text{m}^3 = 500\,000$ litres.
> 6. $2 \cdot 1\,000\,000 = 2\,000\,000\ \text{cm}^3$.
