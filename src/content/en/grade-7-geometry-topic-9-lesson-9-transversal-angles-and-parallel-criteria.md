## Why It Matters

How do you *check* that two lines are parallel? You can't follow them
to infinity. But cross them with a third line and measure two angles:
if the right pair match, the lines are parallel - guaranteed, by a
theorem. That's how a carpenter checks a shelf against the floor, and
it's how geometry turns "never meet" into something you can test with
a protractor.

## The Big Idea

A third line crossing two others - a transversal - makes eight
angles, four at each crossing. Pairs of them get names by position.
Alternate angles sit on opposite sides of the transversal, between
the two lines, like a letter Z. Corresponding angles sit in the same
position at each crossing, like a letter F. Co-interior angles sit on
the same side, between the lines, like a letter C.

The criteria say: if alternate angles are equal, the lines are
parallel. The proof is by contradiction - suppose the lines meet; then
the triangle they form with the transversal would have an exterior
angle equal to an interior one, which the angle-measure axioms
forbid. The other two criteria reduce to the first through vertical
and adjacent angles.

## The Rule

A **transversal** of two lines is a line that intersects both at
different points. With two lines $a$, $b$ and a transversal $c$, the
angles between $a$ and $b$ are **interior**; pairs of them are named:

- **alternate angles** (alternate interior): on opposite sides of $c$;
- **co-interior angles** (same-side interior): on the same side of $c$;
- **corresponding angles**: one interior and one exterior, on the same
  side of $c$, at different crossings.

**Criteria for parallel lines.** Two lines are parallel if, with some
transversal:

1. alternate angles are equal; or
2. corresponding angles are equal; or
3. co-interior angles add up to $180^\circ$.

Each of 2 and 3 follows from 1: a corresponding angle equals the
vertical angle of an alternate one; a co-interior angle is adjacent to
an alternate one.

## Worked Example

Lines $a$ and $b$ are cut by $c$. One pair of alternate angles is $47^\circ$
and $47^\circ$ - are $a$ and $b$ parallel? Then: corresponding angles are
$110^\circ$ and $70^\circ$ - parallel? Then: co-interior angles are $63^\circ$
and $117^\circ$ - parallel?

- **Alternate $47^\circ = 47^\circ$:** criterion 1 - $a \parallel b$.
- **Corresponding $110^\circ$ and $70^\circ$:** not equal - criterion 2
  fails. Are the lines parallel anyway? If they were, corresponding
  angles *would* be equal (next topic). So no: they intersect.
- **Co-interior $63^\circ + 117^\circ = 180^\circ$:** criterion 3 - parallel.

Construction check: to test whether two drawn lines are parallel, cut
them with any line and compare a pair of corresponding angles with a
protractor.

## Common Mistake

Naming the pairs wrongly. Two angles on the same side, one at each
crossing, both *between* the lines, are co-interior - and co-interior
angles of parallel lines are *not* equal (unless both are $90^\circ$),
they sum to $180^\circ$. Testing "equal?" on a co-interior pair, or
"sum to $180$?" on a corresponding pair, gives the wrong verdict. Draw
the Z, F or C through the two angles to identify them.

## The Takeaway

A transversal makes alternate (Z), corresponding (F) and co-interior
(C) pairs. Equal alternate angles, equal corresponding angles, or
co-interior angles summing to $180^\circ$ - any one of these proves the
lines parallel.

## Check Yourself

1. Alternate angles at a transversal are $58^\circ$ and $58^\circ$. Parallel?
2. Corresponding angles are $95^\circ$ and $85^\circ$. Parallel?
3. Co-interior angles are $70^\circ$ and $110^\circ$. Parallel?
4. One interior angle is $40^\circ$; what must its co-interior angle be
   for the lines to be parallel? Its alternate angle?
5. Two lines are both perpendicular to a transversal. Which criterion
   shows they're parallel, and with what angles?
6. Someone concludes two lines are parallel because a pair of
   co-interior angles are both $80^\circ$. What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Yes.
> 2. No.
> 3. Yes.
> 4. $140^\circ$; $40^\circ$.
> 5. Co-interior angles $90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ$ (or corresponding
>    $90^\circ = 90^\circ$).
> 6. Co-interior angles must sum to $180^\circ$; $80 + 80 = 160$, so the
>    lines are not parallel.
