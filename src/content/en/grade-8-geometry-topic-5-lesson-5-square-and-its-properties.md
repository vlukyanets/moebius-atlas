## Why It Matters

The square is where the two roads meet: a rectangle whose sides are
all equal, or a rhombus whose angles are all right. It inherits both
families' properties at once - equal diagonals *and* perpendicular
diagonals *and* angle-bisecting diagonals - and that pile-up of
symmetry is why the square is the tile, the pixel and the unit of
area. Nothing new is proved here; the point is to see the whole family
tree at once.

## The Big Idea

Start from a parallelogram. Add a right angle: rectangle. Add equal
sides instead: rhombus. Add both: square. Each step keeps everything
from before and gains something. So a square has, from the
parallelogram, equal opposite sides and bisecting diagonals; from the
rectangle, four right angles and equal diagonals; from the rhombus,
four equal sides, perpendicular diagonals, and diagonals that bisect
the angles - into $45^\circ$ halves, since the angles are $90^\circ$.

The diagonals of a square cut it into four congruent right isosceles
triangles, and their intersection is the centre of both the circle
through the corners and the circle touching the sides.

## The Rule

A **square** is a rectangle with all sides equal - equivalently, a
rhombus with a right angle.

Properties (all inherited):

- all sides equal, all angles right;
- the diagonals are equal, perpendicular, bisect each other and bisect
  the angles ($45^\circ$ each);
- the diagonals divide the square into four congruent right isosceles
  triangles;
- the intersection of the diagonals is equidistant from the four
  vertices and from the four sides.

The family: every square is a rectangle and a rhombus; every rectangle
and every rhombus is a parallelogram; not every parallelogram is
either. A quadrilateral that is both a rectangle and a rhombus is a
square.

## Worked Example

In square $ABCD$ with diagonals meeting at $O$, $AO = 4$. Find the
diagonals, the angles of $\triangle AOB$, and say why $O$ is the same
distance from every side. Then: which of these are squares - a rhombus
with equal diagonals; a rectangle with perpendicular diagonals; a
parallelogram with a $90^\circ$ angle?

- **Diagonals:** equal, so $AC = BD = 8$.
- **$\triangle AOB$:** $OA = OB$ and $AO \perp BO$: a right isosceles
  triangle, angles $90^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $45^\circ$.
- **Distance to the sides:** $O$ lies on both diagonals, which bisect
  the angles - so $O$ is on the bisector of every angle, hence
  equidistant from the sides of each; all four distances agree.
- **A rhombus with equal diagonals:** a rhombus is a parallelogram;
  equal diagonals make it a rectangle; a rectangle that's a rhombus is
  a square. Yes.
- **A rectangle with perpendicular diagonals:** by the rhombus
  criterion, also a rhombus - a square. Yes.
- **A parallelogram with a right angle:** a rectangle, but its sides
  needn't be equal. Not necessarily.

## Common Mistake

Reading the family tree backwards - "a rectangle is a square with
unequal sides," or "a rhombus is a kind of square." The arrows go
one way: every square is a rectangle and a rhombus, and only *some*
rectangles and rhombi are squares. When a problem says "rectangle,"
don't assume equal sides; when it says "rhombus," don't assume right
angles.

## The Takeaway

A square is a rectangle and a rhombus at once, so it has every
property of both: equal sides, right angles, and diagonals that are
equal, perpendicular, mutually bisecting and angle-bisecting. It's the
most symmetric quadrilateral, and the top of the parallelogram family
tree.

## Check Yourself

1. A square has diagonal $10$. How far is the diagonals' intersection
   from each vertex?
2. What angle does a diagonal of a square make with a side?
3. Is every rhombus a square? Every square a rhombus?
4. A rectangle's diagonals are perpendicular. What is it?
5. The diagonals of a square split it into how many congruent
   triangles, and of what kind?
6. Someone says "$ABCD$ is a rectangle, so $AB = BC$." Why is that
   unjustified?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $5$.
> 2. $45^\circ$.
> 3. No; yes.
> 4. A square.
> 5. Four, right isosceles.
> 6. A rectangle's adjacent sides need not be equal - only a square's
>    are.
