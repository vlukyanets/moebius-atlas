## Why It Matters

A tin can, a pipe, a roll of paper, a water tank, a tree trunk - the
cylinder is the round cousin of the prism, and the most common
manufactured shape there is. How much tin for the can, how much
water in the tank, how long is the label around the roll - all are
the prism formulas with a disc for a base, and the label question
has a neat answer: the side unrolls into a rectangle.

## The Big Idea

Push a disc straight up by a height $h$ and it sweeps out a cylinder:
two equal parallel discs for bases and a curved side. It's a prism
whose base has gone round - and the prism formulas carry over with
$\pi R^2$ for the base area and $2\pi R$ for the perimeter. The curved
side is the surprise: cut it along a vertical line and unroll, and
it's a plain rectangle of height $h$ and width $2\pi R$. So the lateral
area is $2\pi R h$, and the volume, as for any "base pushed up", is
base times height.

## The Rule

A **cylinder** (right circular) is the solid swept by a disc moving
perpendicular to its plane. Its two **bases** are equal discs of
radius $R$ in parallel planes; its **axis** joins their centres; its
**height** $h$ is the distance between the bases; the segments joining
corresponding points of the base circles are the **generators**, all
of length $h$. The lateral surface unrolls into a rectangle $2\pi R$ by
$h$.

$$
S_{\text{lat}} = 2\pi R h, \qquad S_{\text{total}} = 2\pi R h + 2\pi R^2 = 2\pi R (h + R)
$$

$$
V = \pi R^2 h
$$

The **axial section** (through the axis) is a rectangle $2R$ by $h$.

## Worked Example

A can has radius $4$ cm and height $10$ cm. Find the area of its label
(the side), the tin to make it (total surface) and its volume. Then:
a cylinder's axial section is a square of side $6$; find its volume.
Then: a cylindrical tank of diameter $2$ m holds $2\pi$ m³; find its
height.

- **Label:** $2\pi \cdot 4 \cdot 10 = 80\pi$ cm². **Tin:** $80\pi + 2\pi \cdot 16
  = 112\pi$ cm². **Volume:** $\pi \cdot 16 \cdot 10 = 160\pi$ cm³ - about
  half a litre.
- **Square section.** $2R = 6$ and $h = 6$: $R = 3$, $V = \pi \cdot 9 \cdot 6 =
  54\pi$.
- **Tank.** $R = 1$; $\pi \cdot 1 \cdot h = 2\pi$, so $h = 2$ m.

A cylinder whose axial section is a square - height equal to
diameter - is called *equilateral*.

## Common Mistake

Using the diameter as the radius: a $2$ m tank has $R = 1$, and $\pi \cdot
4 \cdot h$ would be four times too big. Since $R$ is squared in the
volume, the error is squared too. The other slip is the lateral area
as $\pi R h$ or $2\pi R^2 h$ - unroll it: a rectangle of width equal to the
*circumference* $2\pi R$ and height $h$, nothing squared.

## The Takeaway

A cylinder is a disc pushed straight up: volume $\pi R^2 h$, lateral
area $2\pi R h$ (the unrolled rectangle), total $2\pi R(h + R)$. The
axial section is a $2R \times h$ rectangle. Halve a diameter before
using it.

## Check Yourself

1. Radius $3$, height $5$. Find the lateral area and volume.
2. A cylinder has diameter $10$ and height $10$. Find its total surface
   area.
3. A cylinder's lateral surface unrolls into a rectangle $12\pi$ by $7$.
   Find $R$, $h$ and the volume.
4. A cylinder has volume $100\pi$ and height $4$. Find its radius.
5. A roll of paper has radius $5$ cm and width $20$ cm; the paper is
   wrapped around a core of radius $2$ cm. Find the volume of paper.
6. Someone computes the volume of a cylinder with diameter $6$ and
   height $2$ as $\pi \cdot 36 \cdot 2 = 72\pi$. Correct it.

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $30\pi$; $45\pi$.
> 2. $2\pi \cdot 5 \cdot 15 = 150\pi$.
> 3. $R = 6$, $h = 7$, $V = 252\pi$.
> 4. $R^2 = 25$, $R = 5$.
> 5. $\pi (25 - 4) \cdot 20 = 420\pi$ cm³.
> 6. $R = 3$: $\pi \cdot 9 \cdot 2 = 18\pi$.
