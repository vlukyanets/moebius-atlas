## Why It Matters

The area of a square against its side. The height a dropped ball has
fallen against time. The stopping distance of a car against its
speed. Each is a quantity that grows with the *square* of another, and
the curve they share - a parabola - is the second shape, after the
straight line, that you'll meet everywhere. It's also the doorway to
square roots and quadratic equations, which is the rest of this year.

## The Big Idea

$y = x^2$: square the input. Table it - $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ gives $0, 1, 4,
9$; and $x = -1, -2, -3$ gives the same $1, 4, 9$, because a negative
squared is positive. So the graph is symmetric: whatever happens on
the right is mirrored on the left. It bottoms out at the origin, since
$x^2$ is never negative, and it rises ever more steeply - the gaps
between $0, 1, 4, 9, 16$ keep growing. That U-shaped curve is the
parabola, with the origin as its vertex.

Reading it backwards is the point of the next topic: for a given
height $y > 0$, there are *two* inputs, $x$ and $-x$, that reach it.

## The Rule

The function $y = x^2$ has:

- **domain:** all numbers; **range:** all $y \geq 0$;
- **graph:** a **parabola** with **vertex** at the origin, opening
  upward; its axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis;
- $y = 0$ only at $x = 0$; $y > 0$ everywhere else;
- **symmetry:** $(-x)^2 = x^2$, so $(x; y)$ on the graph implies $(-x; y)$
  is;
- **decreasing** for $x \leq 0$ and **increasing** for $x \geq 0$;
- opposite values of $x$ give the same $y$; larger $|x|$ gives larger
  $y$.

## Worked Example

Plot $y = x^2$ for $-3 \leq x \leq 3$; read $y$ at $x = 2.5$; find all $x$
with $y = 6.25$; and use the graph to compare $1.7^2$ with $3$.

- **Table:** $x = -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3$ gives $y = 9, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 9$.
  Plot and join with a smooth U.
- **$x = 2.5$:** $y = 6.25$ - on the graph, between $4$ and $9$, closer to
  the middle.
- **$y = 6.25$:** the horizontal line at $6.25$ meets the parabola twice:
  $x = 2.5$ and $x = -2.5$.
- **$1.7^2$ vs $3$:** at $x = 1.7$ the curve is at $2.89$, below the
  height $3$. So $1.7^2 < 3$. (And $1.8^2 = 3.24 > 3$ - so $3$ is reached
  somewhere between $1.7$ and $1.8$, which is where square roots come
  from.)

The ball: after $t$ seconds it has fallen about $5t^2$ metres - the
parabola stretched by $5$.

## Common Mistake

Drawing the parabola with a pointed bottom, or as two straight lines
meeting at the origin. The curve is smooth - it flattens out as it
approaches the vertex - and it's curved everywhere; joining plotted
points with straight segments hides that. Plot extra points near $0$
($x = 0.5$, $y = 0.25$) to see the flatness. And the range is $y \geq 0$,
not "all numbers": the parabola never dips below the axis.

## The Takeaway

$y = x^2$ draws a parabola: vertex at the origin, symmetric about the
$y$-axis, never below it, falling on the left and rising on the right.
Each positive height is hit at two opposite $x$'s - which is exactly
what a square root will name.

## Check Yourself

1. Find $y$ for $x = -4$ and $x = 4$ on $y = x^2$.
2. What's the range of $y = x^2$? Its lowest point?
3. Find all $x$ with $x^2 = 49$.
4. On which side of $0$ is $y = x^2$ increasing?
5. Does $(-3; -9)$ lie on the graph of $y = x^2$?
6. Someone says $y = x^2$ has range "all numbers, since $x$ can be
   anything." What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $16$ and $16$.
> 2. $y \geq 0$; the vertex $(0; 0)$.
> 3. $x = 7$ or $x = -7$.
> 4. For $x \geq 0$.
> 5. No - $(-3)^2 = 9$, not $-9$.
> 6. The outputs are squares and never negative; the range is $y \geq 0$.
