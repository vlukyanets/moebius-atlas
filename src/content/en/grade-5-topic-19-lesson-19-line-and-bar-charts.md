## Why It Matters

A table of the temperature every day for a month is thirty numbers, and
you'd have to read all thirty to say whether it got warmer. Draw them as
a line, and the answer is visible at a glance - the line goes up. A chart
turns a list of numbers into a shape, and shapes are what eyes are good
at. Weather reports, sports stats, election results, your own marks -
they all get charted for that one reason.

## The Big Idea

A chart is a scale, or two of them, with the numbers turned into
lengths. In a bar chart, each thing gets a bar, and the bar is as tall as
its number - taller bar, bigger number, no reading required. In a line
chart, each moment in time gets a point at the right height, and the
points are joined in order - so the line rises where the number grew and
falls where it shrank. Reading either one is exactly reading a scale:
find what one division is worth, then look where the bar ends or the
point sits.

## The Rule

A **bar chart** compares separate things. Along the bottom go the things
being compared - months, teams, sports; up the side goes a scale. Each
thing gets a bar whose height, read on the scale, is its number. Bars
don't touch and their order usually doesn't matter.

A **line chart** shows how one quantity changes over time. Along the
bottom goes time - days, hours, years - in order; up the side goes the
scale. Each moment gets a point, and the points are joined by segments.
A rising segment means growth, a falling one means a drop, a flat one
means no change.

To read a chart: find the value of a division on the side scale, find the
bar top or the point, and read across. To draw one: choose a scale that
fits the biggest number, mark the axis, then place every bar or point.

## Worked Example

A class counted the books read by four pupils in a term: Anna $12$, Bohdan
$7$, Vira $15$, Danylo $9$. Draw a bar chart and answer: who read the
most, and how many more than Bohdan?

- **Choose the scale.** The biggest number is $15$, so a side scale from
  $0$ to $16$ with one division worth $2$ works: eight divisions.
- **Draw the bars.** Four bars along the bottom, one per pupil. Anna's
  reaches $12$ - six divisions; Bohdan's reaches $7$ - three and a half;
  Vira's reaches $15$; Danylo's $9$.
- **Read it.** The tallest bar is Vira's: $15$ books. Bohdan's is the
  shortest: $7$. The difference is $15 - 7 = 8$ books.

Now a line chart of the temperature at noon over five days: $14, 17, 17,
12, 15$ degrees. Five points, joined in order. The line rises from day $1$
to day $2$, is flat from day $2$ to day $3$, drops sharply to day $4$ -
the coldest day - and rises again to day $5$.

## Common Mistake

Reading a bar against the wrong division. If one division is worth $2$
and a bar ends three divisions up, the value is $6$, not $3$. The side
scale is a scale like any other: work out what a division is worth before
you read a single bar - and never assume it's $1$.

## The Takeaway

A bar chart compares separate things by the height of their bars; a line
chart shows one thing changing over time by a line that rises and falls.
Both are read like a scale: find the value of a division first.

## Check Yourself

1. Which chart fits better: the number of goals scored by each of five
   teams, or the temperature at each hour of one day?
2. On a bar chart one division is worth $5$. A bar reaches four divisions.
   What's its value?
3. A line chart goes flat between Tuesday and Wednesday. What happened to
   the quantity?
4. The heights of four bars are $3, 8, 8, 5$ divisions, one division
   worth $10$. Which values are equal, and what are they?
5. A line chart of a shop's daily sales over a week has its highest point
   on Saturday and lowest on Monday. On which day did the shop sell the
   most?
6. Someone reads a bar that ends three divisions up as "$3$" on a chart
   where one division is $20$. What's the right value?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Bar chart for the teams - separate things; line chart for the
>    temperature - one thing over time.
> 2. $4 \cdot 5 = 20$.
> 3. It didn't change.
> 4. The second and third bars, both $80$.
> 5. Saturday.
> 6. $3 \cdot 20 = 60$.
