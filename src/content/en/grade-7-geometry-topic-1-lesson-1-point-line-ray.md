## Why It Matters

Geometry starts over this year - on purpose. The point, line and ray
you met in grade 5 come back, but now the game is different: instead
of trusting pictures, you'll agree on a few plain statements about
points and lines and then *prove* everything else from them. That's
how geometry has been done since Euclid, and this topic sets down the
first pieces: the simplest figures and the first facts about them.

## The Big Idea

A point has no size; a line has no thickness and no ends; a plane has
no edges. None of them can be drawn - what you draw is a dot, a
stroke, a page, stand-ins for the real thing. Geometry is the study of
these ideal figures, and its objects are defined by how they relate:
a point lies on a line or it doesn't; two lines share a point or they
don't.

The first facts are ones nobody argues with. Any line has points on it
and points off it. Through two points there's one line and only one.
Two lines that share a point share no other. From these, everything
about lines will follow.

## The Rule

The basic figures of plane geometry are the **point**, the **line** and
the **plane**. Points are named by capital letters ($A$, $B$), lines by
small letters ($a$, $b$) or by two of their points ($AB$).

**Basic properties of belonging:**

- Whatever the line, there are points that belong to it and points
  that do not.
- Through any two points there passes exactly one line.

Consequence: two distinct lines either have exactly one common point
(they **intersect**) or none.

A **ray** is the part of a line lying on one side of a given point of
it, including that point - its **origin**. A point of a line divides it
into two **complementary rays**. The ray with origin $O$ through $A$ is
written $OA$.

A **geometric figure** is any set of points. A **segment** is the part of
a line between two points, with those points as its ends.

## Worked Example

Points $A$, $B$, $C$ lie on a line in that order, and $D$ is off the
line. How many lines pass through pairs of these four points? Name
the complementary rays with origin $B$ on the line.

- **Pairs among $A$, $B$, $C$** all lie on the one line $AB$ - that's one
  line.
- **Pairs with $D$:** $DA$, $DB$, $DC$ are three different lines - each
  passes through $D$ and one point of the line, and two of them can't
  coincide, or $D$ would be on the line $AB$.
- **Total:** $4$ lines.
- **Rays from $B$:** $BA$ and $BC$ - opposite directions, complementary.

Can two lines have two common points? No: through two points there's
only one line, so they'd be the same line.

## Common Mistake

Arguing from the drawing. "These two lines don't meet on my page, so
they don't meet" - but lines have no ends, and they may meet far off
the paper. In this course a picture *suggests*; only the agreed
properties and what follows from them *prove*. Whenever you claim
something, ask which property or theorem it rests on.

## The Takeaway

Points, lines and planes are ideal figures known by their relations.
Through two points there's exactly one line, so two lines meet at most
once. A point on a line cuts it into two complementary rays. From
here on, facts are proved, not seen.

## Check Yourself

1. How many lines pass through one point? Through two?
2. Points $M$, $N$, $K$ don't lie on one line. How many lines pass
   through pairs of them?
3. Can two distinct lines have three common points?
4. The point $O$ lies on the line $a$. How many rays with origin $O$ lie
   on $a$?
5. Are the rays $AB$ and $BA$ the same? The segments $AB$ and $BA$?
6. Someone says two lines are "obviously" different because they look
   different on the sketch, though both pass through $P$ and $Q$. What's
   wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Infinitely many; exactly one.
> 2. Three.
> 3. No - two common points already force them to coincide.
> 4. Two, complementary.
> 5. No - different origins; yes.
> 6. Through two points there's only one line; they must be the same
>    line, however the sketch looks.
