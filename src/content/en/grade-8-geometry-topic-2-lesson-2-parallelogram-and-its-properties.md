## Why It Matters

Push a rectangular frame sideways and it leans into a parallelogram -
the shape of a folding gate, a scissor lift, the shadow of a window.
It keeps a surprising amount of the rectangle's order: opposite sides
stay equal, opposite angles stay equal, the diagonals still cut each
other in half. Those three properties, proved with congruent
triangles, are the foundation for every special quadrilateral that
follows.

## The Big Idea

A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides. Draw a diagonal: it's
a transversal of both pairs, so it makes equal alternate angles at
its two ends - and the two triangles it creates are congruent by ASA
(the diagonal is their common side). Everything follows from that
congruence: the opposite sides match up, and the opposite angles do
too.

For the diagonals, draw both. The two small triangles on opposite
sides of the crossing are congruent - equal sides (the opposite sides
of the parallelogram) and equal angles (alternate angles again) - so
the pieces of each diagonal match: the crossing point is the midpoint
of both.

The converses are also true, and they're the criteria for recognising
a parallelogram: two pairs of equal opposite sides; one pair of sides
both parallel and equal; diagonals that bisect each other.

## The Rule

A **parallelogram** is a quadrilateral whose opposite sides are
pairwise parallel.

**Properties.** In a parallelogram:

1. opposite sides are equal;
2. opposite angles are equal; adjacent angles sum to $180^\circ$;
3. the diagonals bisect each other - their intersection is the
   midpoint of each.

*Proof of 1 and 2.* In $ABCD$, the diagonal $AC$ gives $\angle BAC = \angle
DCA$ and $\angle BCA = \angle DAC$ (alternate angles for the two pairs of
parallels). With $AC$ common, $\triangle ABC = \triangle CDA$ by ASA; so $AB
= CD$, $BC = DA$, $\angle B = \angle D$; and $\angle A = \angle C$ as sums of the
equal parts. $\square$

**Criteria (converses).** A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if:
opposite sides are pairwise equal; **or** two opposite sides are equal
and parallel; **or** the diagonals bisect each other.

The **altitude** of a parallelogram is a perpendicular between two
opposite sides.

## Worked Example

In parallelogram $ABCD$, $AB = 7$, $BC = 4$, $\angle A = 65^\circ$. Find the
other sides and angles. Then: the diagonals of $ABCD$ meet at $O$, $AO =
5$, $BO = 3$ - find $AC$ and $BD$. Then: is a quadrilateral with sides $6$,
$4$, $6$, $4$ in that order necessarily a parallelogram?

- **Sides:** $CD = AB = 7$, $DA = BC = 4$.
- **Angles:** $\angle C = \angle A = 65^\circ$; $\angle B = \angle D = 180^\circ -
  65^\circ = 115^\circ$. Check: $65 + 115 + 65 + 115 = 360$.
- **Diagonals:** $AC = 2 \cdot AO = 10$, $BD = 2 \cdot BO = 6$.
- **Sides $6, 4, 6, 4$:** opposite sides equal in pairs - by the first
  criterion, yes, a parallelogram.

Perimeter of $ABCD$: $2(7 + 4) = 22$.

## Common Mistake

Assuming the diagonals are equal, or that they bisect the angles. In
a general parallelogram the diagonals have *different* lengths ($10$
and $6$ above), and neither bisects the angle at its vertex. Those
extra properties belong to rectangles and rhombi, coming next. What
every parallelogram guarantees is that the diagonals bisect *each
other* - the crossing halves both.

## The Takeaway

Two pairs of parallel sides force equal opposite sides, equal opposite
angles and diagonals that cut each other in half - via congruent
triangles from a diagonal. Any one of the converse conditions proves
a quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

## Check Yourself

1. In parallelogram $KLMN$, $\angle K = 48^\circ$. Find the other three
   angles.
2. A parallelogram has perimeter $30$ and one side $9$. Find the
   adjacent side.
3. The diagonals of a parallelogram are $12$ and $8$. How far is their
   intersection from each vertex?
4. Two angles of a parallelogram differ by $40^\circ$. Find all four.
5. A quadrilateral's diagonals bisect each other. What can you
   conclude?
6. Someone claims the diagonals of every parallelogram are equal. Give
   a counterexample from this topic.

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $132^\circ$, $48^\circ$, $132^\circ$.
> 2. $6$.
> 3. $6$ and $4$ (halves of each diagonal).
> 4. $70^\circ$, $110^\circ$, $70^\circ$, $110^\circ$.
> 5. It's a parallelogram.
> 6. The worked example: diagonals $10$ and $6$.
