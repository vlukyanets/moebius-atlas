## Why It Matters

Two lines cross and make four angles. Measure one, and you know all
four: the two across from each other are equal, and each pair of
neighbours adds to $180^\circ$. That's the vertical angles theorem, the
second one you'll prove, and it's the reason you can talk about *the*
angle between two lines at all - because the crossing gives you only
two different sizes, not four.

## The Big Idea

Look at a crossing of two lines like a pair of scissors. The blades
open to the same angle on both sides of the pivot - the angle between
the tips equals the angle between the handles. Those two are vertical
angles: they share a vertex, and the sides of one are the extensions
of the sides of the other.

Why are they equal? Each of them is adjacent to the *same* third
angle - the one between them. Two angles that both add to $180^\circ$
with the same angle must be equal to each other. That's the whole
proof, and it rides on the adjacent-angles theorem.

## The Rule

Two angles are **vertical** if the sides of one are complementary
rays of the sides of the other. Two intersecting lines form two pairs
of vertical angles.

**Theorem.** Vertical angles are equal.

*Proof.* Let $\angle 1$ and $\angle 3$ be vertical, and $\angle 2$ the angle
adjacent to both. Then $\angle 1 + \angle 2 = 180^\circ$ and $\angle 3 +
\angle 2 = 180^\circ$, so $\angle 1 = 180^\circ - \angle 2 = \angle 3$. $\square$

The **angle between two intersecting lines** is the smaller of the
angles they form (or $90^\circ$ if they're equal). It's never more than
$90^\circ$. If it *is* $90^\circ$, all four angles are right.

So at a crossing, one angle determines the rest: the vertical one
equals it, and the two adjacent ones are its supplement to $180^\circ$.

## Worked Example

Two lines intersect, and one of the four angles is $52^\circ$. Find the
other three and the angle between the lines. Then: the sum of two
vertical angles is $140^\circ$ - find all four angles.

- **Vertical to $52^\circ$:** also $52^\circ$.
- **Adjacent to $52^\circ$:** $180^\circ - 52^\circ = 128^\circ$ - and there
  are two of those, vertical to each other.
- **Angle between the lines:** the smaller, $52^\circ$.
- **Second:** two vertical angles are equal, so each is $70^\circ$; the
  other two are $110^\circ$ each.

One more: one angle at a crossing is $3$ times its neighbour. Neighbours
are adjacent: $x + 3x = 180$, $x = 45$. Angles $45^\circ$, $135^\circ$, $45^\circ$,
$135^\circ$.

## Common Mistake

Mixing up which pair is which. The angles *across* the crossing from
each other are vertical and equal; the angles *next to* each other are
adjacent and sum to $180^\circ$. Calling two adjacent angles "vertical"
and setting them equal gives $90^\circ$ for everything, which is only
right when the lines are perpendicular. Trace the sides: vertical
angles' sides are each other's extensions.

## The Takeaway

At a crossing of two lines, opposite angles are equal and neighbouring
angles add to $180^\circ$ - the first from the second, in a two-line
proof. One angle fixes all four, and the smaller of them is the angle
between the lines.

## Check Yourself

1. One angle at a crossing is $117^\circ$. Find the other three.
2. What's the angle between the lines in question 1?
3. Two vertical angles sum to $86^\circ$. Find the four angles.
4. One of the angles at a crossing is $5$ times its adjacent angle.
   Find all four.
5. If one angle at a crossing is $90^\circ$, what are the others, and
   why?
6. Someone says two angles at a crossing are vertical and sets them
   equal, getting $180^\circ$ each. Which pair did they really pick?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $117^\circ$, $63^\circ$, $63^\circ$.
> 2. $63^\circ$.
> 3. $43^\circ$, $43^\circ$, $137^\circ$, $137^\circ$.
> 4. $30^\circ$, $150^\circ$, $30^\circ$, $150^\circ$.
> 5. All $90^\circ$: the vertical one by equality, the adjacent ones as
>    $180^\circ - 90^\circ$.
> 6. An adjacent pair - those sum to $180^\circ$; vertical angles are the
>    ones across the vertex.
