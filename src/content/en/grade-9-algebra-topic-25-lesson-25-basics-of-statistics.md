## Why It Matters

A survey of $1000$ people, the marks of a whole class, the daily
temperatures of a year - raw data is a pile of numbers, and by
itself it says nothing. Statistics is the craft of turning the pile
into a picture and a few summary numbers you can actually reason
about. This topic is the first half of it: how data is collected,
tabulated and drawn, so that the numbers of the next topic - mode,
median, mean - have something to describe.

## The Big Idea

Data comes as a list of observations - one per person, per day, per
item. The first move is always to *count*: how many times did each
value occur? That's a frequency table, and it already compresses a
list of a hundred marks into six rows. The second move is to *draw*
it - a bar chart for a handful of values, a histogram for a range
split into intervals, a pie chart for shares of a whole - so that the
shape of the data is visible at a glance: where it piles up, how far
it spreads, whether it's lopsided. Everything in statistics starts
from these two moves.

## The Rule

**Statistics** collects, organises, presents and analyses data.

- A **population** is the whole set of objects under study; a
  **sample** is the part actually observed.
- Data is gathered by **observation**, **experiment** or **survey**.

**Frequency table:** for each distinct value, its **frequency** (how
many times it occurs). The **relative frequency** is the frequency
divided by the total number of observations; relative frequencies
sum to $1$ (or $100\%$).

**Presentation:**

- **bar chart** - a bar per value, height = frequency;
- **histogram** - values grouped into intervals, a bar per interval;
- **pie chart** - a sector per value, angle proportional to relative
  frequency ($360^\circ \cdot$ relative frequency);
- **line graph** - for data ordered in time.

## Worked Example

The marks of $20$ students on a test were: $7, 9, 8, 10, 7, 6, 8, 9, 8, 7, 10,
8, 9, 8, 7, 6, 8, 9, 8, 10$. Make a frequency table with relative
frequencies, and find the pie-chart angle for the mark $8$.

- **Count.** $6$: $2$ times; $7$: $4$; $8$: $7$; $9$: $4$; $10$: $3$. Check:
  $2 + 4 + 7 + 4 + 3 = 20$. Good.
- **Relative frequencies.** $6$: $\frac{2}{20} = 0.1$; $7$: $0.2$; $8$: $0.35$;
  $9$: $0.2$; $10$: $0.15$. Sum: $1$. Good.
- **Pie angle for $8$:** $360^\circ \cdot 0.35 = 126^\circ$.

The bar chart would show a peak at $8$ with a gentle fall either
side - the shape you'd expect from a test of the right difficulty.

Grouping: the heights of $30$ students, from $150$ to $180$ cm, are
better tabulated by intervals $[150; 160)$, $[160; 170)$, $[170; 180]$
than by every centimetre - that's a histogram.

## Common Mistake

Frequencies that don't add up. If the counts sum to $19$ for $20$
students, a value was missed - always check the total against the
number of observations, and the relative frequencies against $1$. The
other slip is a pie chart with angles proportional to the *values*
($6, 7, 8, \dots$) rather than to their frequencies: the slice for mark
$8$ is big because seven students got it, not because $8$ is a big
number.

## The Takeaway

Count each value into a frequency table; divide by the total for
relative frequencies, which sum to $1$; draw a bar chart, histogram
or pie chart to see the shape. Check the totals. Data has to be
organised before it can be summarised.

## Check Yourself

1. What is the difference between a population and a sample?
2. Data: $3, 5, 3, 4, 5, 5, 3, 4$. Make a frequency table.
3. For the same data, find the relative frequency of $5$.
4. What pie-chart angle corresponds to a relative frequency of $0.25$?
5. When is a histogram used instead of a bar chart?
6. A frequency table for $25$ observations has relative frequencies
   $0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2$. What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. The whole set studied versus the part actually observed.
> 2. $3$: $3$; $4$: $2$; $5$: $3$.
> 3. $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$.
> 4. $90^\circ$.
> 5. When there are many distinct values, grouped into intervals.
> 6. They sum to $1.1$, not $1$ - a count is wrong.
