## Why It Matters

The circle came with $\pi$ in grade 6; now it comes with theorems.
Where does a line cut a circle, and how many times? What does the
centre do to a chord? The answers are the first geometry of curves,
and they're what compasses, wheels, arches and every "equidistant"
argument depend on. Two topics from now, they'll put a circle inside a
triangle and one around it.

## The Big Idea

A circle is every point at one distance from the centre; a disc is
the circle plus its inside. Chords join two points of the circle, and
the longest chord passes through the centre - a diameter. Drop a
perpendicular from the centre to a chord: the two right triangles it
makes with the endpoints have equal hypotenuses (both radii) and a
common leg, so they're congruent, and the foot is the midpoint. The
perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord - and the
perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre.

A line and a circle can meet twice, once, or not at all, depending
on whether the centre's distance to the line is less than, equal to,
or greater than the radius.

## The Rule

A **circle** with centre $O$ and radius $r$ is the set of points at
distance $r$ from $O$. A **disc** is the set of points at distance at
most $r$ from $O$ - the circle together with its interior.

A **chord** is a segment joining two points of the circle; a
**diameter** is a chord through the centre, of length $2r$; an **arc**
is a part of the circle between two of its points.

**Theorem.** The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects
the chord; the diameter perpendicular to a chord bisects it.

**Theorem.** The perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through
the centre.

**Line and circle.** Let $d$ be the distance from the centre to a
line. If $d < r$, the line meets the circle in two points (a
**secant**); if $d = r$, in one point; if $d > r$, not at all.

## Worked Example

A chord $AB = 16$ cm lies in a circle of radius $10$ cm. Find the
distance from the centre to the chord. Then: in a circle of radius $5$,
a line is $5$ from the centre - how many common points? And a line $3$
from the centre?

- **Perpendicular from $O$ to $AB$** meets it at the midpoint $M$: $AM =
  8$.
- **$\triangle OMA$ is right** at $M$ with $OA = 10$, $AM = 8$. Its other
  leg $OM$ is the distance - and next year's theorem gives $OM = 6$
  ($6^2 + 8^2 = 10^2$). For now: the $6, 8, 10$ triangle is right, as
  a construction check confirms.
- **Distance $5 = r$:** one common point. **Distance $3 < 5$:** two.

Two equal chords in a circle are the same distance from the centre -
the perpendiculars from $O$ give congruent right triangles
(hypotenuse and leg).

## Common Mistake

Confusing circle and disc. The circle is the *rim* - a curve; the disc
is the *plate* - a region. "A point inside the circle" is inside the
disc but *not on the circle*. And a chord is a segment, not the arc it
cuts off: the chord is straight, the arc is curved, and they're
different lengths.

## The Takeaway

A circle is the rim, a disc the whole plate. The perpendicular from
the centre to a chord bisects it, and a chord's perpendicular bisector
goes through the centre. A line meets a circle twice, once or never,
according to whether its distance from the centre is under, at or over
the radius.

## Check Yourself

1. What's the longest chord of a circle of radius $7$?
2. A chord is $12$ and the perpendicular from the centre bisects it.
   How far is its midpoint from each end?
3. A line is $8$ from the centre of a circle of radius $6$. Common
   points?
4. Where does the perpendicular bisector of any chord pass?
5. Two chords are equal. What can you say about their distances from
   the centre?
6. Someone says a point at distance $3$ from the centre of a circle of
   radius $5$ "lies on the circle." Correct them.

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. The diameter, $14$.
> 2. $6$.
> 3. None.
> 4. Through the centre.
> 5. Equal.
> 6. It lies inside the disc; on the circle means distance exactly $5$.
