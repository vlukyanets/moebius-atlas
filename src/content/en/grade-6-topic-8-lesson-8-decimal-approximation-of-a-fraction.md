## Why It Matters

$\frac{2}{3}$ of a metre of ribbon is $0.666\ldots$ m, and no shop
assistant can cut "$0.666$ recurring." They cut $0.67$ m, or $0.7$ m,
depending on how careful they need to be. A repeating decimal is exact
but endless; for measuring, paying and comparing, you need a decimal
that stops. Rounding a fraction to a chosen number of places gives you
one - and tells you how far off it is.

## The Big Idea

You already know how to round a decimal: look at the next digit, round
up on $5$ or more, drop the rest. A fraction is just a decimal you
haven't finished writing - so divide out one digit *past* the place you
want, and round. For $\frac{2}{3}$ to hundredths, you need the
thousandths digit as the decider: $0.666\ldots$, decider $6$, round up
to $0.67$.

The result isn't equal to the fraction, so it gets the "approximately
equal" sign, and it can be too big or too small: $0.67$ is a little more
than $\frac{2}{3}$, $0.66$ would be a little less.

## The Rule

To find the **decimal approximation** of a fraction to a given place:

1. divide the numerator by the denominator, going one digit past the
   place you need;
2. round the result to that place.

$$
\frac{2}{3} = 0.666\ldots \approx 0.67 \text{ (to hundredths)}, \qquad
\frac{5}{7} = 0.714\ldots \approx 0.7 \text{ (to tenths)}
$$

The approximation is **by deficiency** (rounded down) if it's smaller
than the fraction, and **by excess** (rounded up) if it's larger.

If the fraction converts to a terminating decimal with no more places
than needed, it's exact - no rounding, and $=$ rather than $\approx$.

## Worked Example

Find the decimal approximation of $\frac{5}{7}$ to hundredths.

- **Divide to thousandths:** $5 : 7 = 0.714\ldots$ ($50 : 7 = 7$ rem. $1$;
  $10 : 7 = 1$ rem. $3$; $30 : 7 = 4$ rem. $2$).
- **Round to hundredths:** the decider is the thousandths digit, $4$ -
  round down. $\frac{5}{7} \approx 0.71$.
- **Deficiency or excess?** $0.71 < 0.714\ldots$, so this is by
  deficiency.

The ribbon: $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.67$ m to hundredths - by excess; to
tenths, $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.7$ m, also by excess.

And an exact case: $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$ to thousandths - no
approximation needed. To hundredths, $\frac{3}{8} \approx 0.38$.

## Common Mistake

Rounding a repeating decimal by cutting it off. $\frac{2}{3}$ "to
hundredths" written as $0.66$ is a truncation, not a rounding: the
decider $6$ demanded a round-up to $0.67$. Truncating always rounds
down, which is only right when the decider is below $5$. Divide out one
extra digit and let it decide.

## The Takeaway

A fraction's decimal approximation is its decimal expansion divided out
one digit further than needed and then rounded. Say whether the
approximation is by deficiency or by excess; use $\approx$, since it
isn't equal to the fraction.

## Check Yourself

1. Approximate $\frac{1}{6}$ to hundredths.
2. Approximate $\frac{4}{9}$ to tenths. By deficiency or excess?
3. Approximate $\frac{7}{11}$ to thousandths.
4. Write $\frac{5}{16}$ to hundredths. Is it exact?
5. A $\frac{4}{7}$-litre bottle - how many millilitres, to the nearest
   millilitre?
6. Someone approximates $\frac{2}{3}$ to hundredths as $0.66$. What's
   wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $0.1666\ldots \approx 0.17$.
> 2. $0.444\ldots \approx 0.4$, by deficiency.
> 3. $0.636363\ldots \approx 0.636$.
> 4. $0.3125 \approx 0.31$; not exact - it needs four places.
> 5. $\frac{4}{7} = 0.5714\ldots$ l $\approx 571$ ml.
> 6. That's truncation; the decider $6$ rounds up to $0.67$.
