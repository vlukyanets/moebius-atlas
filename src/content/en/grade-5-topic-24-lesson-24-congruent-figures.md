## Why It Matters

A key and its copy; two tiles from the same box; the left and right halves
of a folded paper heart. If they weren't exactly the same shape and size,
the copy wouldn't turn the lock and the tiles wouldn't fit. Geometry
needs a precise word for "exactly the same, even if it's been moved,
turned or flipped" - and that word is *congruent*.

## The Big Idea

Cut a triangle out of paper. Slide it across the table, spin it round,
even flip it over - it's still the same triangle. Now cut a second one
and lay it on the first. If you can line it up so that they cover each
other exactly, edge on edge and corner on corner, the two are congruent.
If there's always a bit sticking out, they aren't.

That test - lay one on the other - is the whole definition. It doesn't
matter where the figures sit or which way they face; only whether one
can be made to coincide with the other.

## The Rule

Two figures are **congruent** (equal) if they can be laid one on the
other so that they coincide exactly. Written $\triangle ABC =
\triangle MNK$, or simply "figure $F$ equals figure $G$."

What follows from coinciding:

- congruent segments have equal lengths, and segments of equal length are
  congruent;
- congruent angles have equal measures;
- in congruent triangles all the matching sides are equal and all the
  matching angles are equal;
- congruent figures have equal perimeters.

The opposite is not always true: two figures with the same perimeter need
not be congruent - a $3 \times 5$ rectangle and a $2 \times 6$ rectangle
both have perimeter $16$ and clearly don't coincide.

## Worked Example

$\triangle ABC = \triangle MNK$, with $AB = 5$ cm, $BC = 7$ cm, $AC = 9$ cm
and $\angle B = 80^\circ$. Find the sides of $\triangle MNK$, its perimeter
and $\angle N$.

- **Match the vertices.** The letters are written in matching order: $A$
  goes with $M$, $B$ with $N$, $C$ with $K$.
- **Match the sides:** $MN = AB = 5$ cm, $NK = BC = 7$ cm, $MK = AC =
  9$ cm.
- **Perimeter:** the same as for $ABC$, $5 + 7 + 9 = 21$ cm.
- **Match the angle:** $\angle N$ sits at the vertex matching $B$, so
  $\angle N = \angle B = 80^\circ$.

Now two rectangles: one $4$ cm by $6$ cm, another $6$ cm by $4$ cm. Turn
the second one a quarter-turn and it coincides with the first. They're
congruent - orientation doesn't count.

## Common Mistake

Deciding that two figures are congruent because a number matches. Two
triangles with perimeter $21$ cm, or two rectangles with perimeter
$16$ cm, might be very different shapes. One matching number is a hint,
not a proof; congruence means *everything* matches, which is what laying
one on the other checks.

## The Takeaway

Figures are congruent when one can be laid over the other to coincide
exactly - position, turning and flipping don't matter. Congruent figures
share every measurement; sharing one measurement doesn't make figures
congruent.

## Check Yourself

1. Are two segments of $4$ cm and $40$ mm congruent?
2. $\triangle ABC = \triangle DEF$, $AB = 6$ cm, $BC = 4$ cm, $CA = 7$ cm.
   Find $DE$, $EF$, $FD$.
3. Two squares have sides $5$ cm. Are they congruent?
4. Two rectangles have perimeter $20$ cm. Must they be congruent? Give an
   example.
5. $\triangle PQR = \triangle XYZ$ and $\angle Q = 35^\circ$. Which angle
   of $XYZ$ is $35^\circ$?
6. Someone says a triangle and its mirror image "can't be congruent
   because it's flipped." Where's the mistake?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Yes - $40$ mm is $4$ cm, and equal segments are congruent.
> 2. $DE = 6$ cm, $EF = 4$ cm, $FD = 7$ cm.
> 3. Yes - a square is fixed by its side.
> 4. No: $4 \times 6$ and $3 \times 7$ both have perimeter $20$ and don't
>    coincide.
> 5. $\angle Y$ - it matches $Q$.
> 6. Flipping is allowed: if the flipped copy can be laid on the original
>    to coincide, the two are congruent.
