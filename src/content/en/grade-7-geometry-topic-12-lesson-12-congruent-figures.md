## Why It Matters

Two triangles are equal if their six parts match. But checking six
things is a lot, and for a general figure - a pentagon, a curve -
"parts" isn't even clear. Grade 5 said equal figures are ones that
can be laid on each other. This topic makes that idea precise, ties it
to the six-part definition for triangles, and sets up the question
the next four topics answer: how *few* matching parts are enough.

## The Big Idea

Cut a figure from paper and move it - slide it, turn it, flip it over.
It lands somewhere else, unchanged: every distance between its points
is what it was. Two figures are equal if one can be moved onto the
other so that they coincide exactly. A movement that keeps distances
is called a motion, and it's the precise version of "picking up the
paper."

For triangles, that's the same as the six-part definition: if the
triangles coincide after a motion, every side lands on a side and
every angle on an angle; and conversely, if the six parts agree, the
motion that carries one triangle onto the other exists. The two
definitions agree, and the one you use is whichever is handier.

## The Rule

A **motion** is a transformation of the plane that preserves
distances: any two points $X$, $Y$ go to points $X'$, $Y'$ with $X'Y' =
XY$. Translations, rotations and reflections are motions. A motion
carries a segment to an equal segment, an angle to an equal angle, a
triangle to an equal triangle.

Two figures are **equal** (congruent) if there is a motion carrying one
onto the other.

For triangles this coincides with the earlier definition: $\triangle
ABC = \triangle A_1B_1C_1$ exactly when the corresponding sides and
angles are equal.

Properties of equality of figures: every figure equals itself; if $F
= G$ then $G = F$; if $F = G$ and $G = H$ then $F = H$.

Equal figures have equal perimeters, and (once area is defined) equal
areas.

## Worked Example

A triangle is reflected in a line to give a second triangle. Are they
equal? Then: two rectangles are $3 \times 7$ and $4 \times 6$ - equal
perimeters, but are they equal figures? Then: $\triangle ABC$ is moved
by a motion to $\triangle A'B'C'$ with $AB = 4$, $\angle C = 70^\circ$ - what
are $A'B'$ and $\angle C'$?

- **Reflection is a motion** - it keeps every distance - so the
  triangles are equal. A flipped copy still counts.
- **Rectangles:** both have perimeter $20$. Can one be moved onto the
  other? No - a motion preserves the side lengths, and $3 \neq 4, 6$.
  Not equal.
- **After a motion:** distances and angles are preserved, so $A'B' =
  4$ and $\angle C' = 70^\circ$.

Equal figures share every measurement; a single shared measurement
doesn't make figures equal.

## Common Mistake

Concluding equality from one matching quantity - equal perimeters,
equal areas, one equal side. Equality means *coincidence under a
motion*, which forces every length and angle to match. And the
reverse slip: refusing equality because a figure is "upside down" or
"mirrored." Flipping is a motion; a triangle and its mirror image are
equal.

## The Takeaway

Figures are equal when a distance-preserving motion carries one onto
the other - sliding, turning and flipping all allowed. For triangles
that's the same as six matching parts; for any figure, it means every
measurement matches.

## Check Yourself

1. Is a translation a motion? A reflection? A stretch that doubles all
   lengths?
2. $\triangle ABC$ is rotated to $\triangle A'B'C'$. What's $B'C'$ if $BC =
   9$?
3. Two squares have the same perimeter. Are they equal? Two rectangles?
4. Two triangles have equal perimeters. Must they be equal?
5. A figure is equal to a second, which is equal to a third. Is the
   first equal to the third?
6. Someone says a triangle and its mirror image aren't equal "because
   you'd have to flip the paper." What's wrong?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Yes; yes; no - it changes distances.
> 2. $9$.
> 3. Yes; not necessarily.
> 4. No.
> 5. Yes.
> 6. Flipping is a motion - it preserves distances - so they are equal.
