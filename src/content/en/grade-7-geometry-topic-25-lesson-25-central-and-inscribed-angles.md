## Why It Matters

Stand on the rim of a circular arena and look at two seats across the
way; walk along the rim and look again - the angle between them
doesn't change. And it's exactly half what someone at the centre
would see. That's the inscribed angle theorem, the first surprising
theorem about circles: an angle with its vertex on the circle is half
the central angle over the same arc. It's the key to angles in circles
for the rest of school, and to why every angle in a semicircle is
right.

## The Big Idea

A central angle has its vertex at the centre; it measures the arc it
cuts off. An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and its
sides are chords. Compare them over the same arc: the inscribed one is
always half.

Why? Take the easy case first - one side of the inscribed angle is a
diameter. Then the centre lies on that side, and joining the centre to
the other end of the angle makes an isosceles triangle (two radii).
The central angle is an exterior angle of that triangle, so it equals
the two equal base angles together - twice the inscribed angle. The
other cases split or subtract along a diameter through the vertex.

## The Rule

A **central angle** is an angle with its vertex at the centre of the
circle. Its measure equals the **degree measure of the arc** it cuts
off; the full circle is $360^\circ$.

An **inscribed angle** is an angle whose vertex lies on the circle and
whose sides intersect the circle - its sides are chords.

**Theorem.** An inscribed angle equals half the central angle over the
same arc - half the arc it subtends:

$$
\angle ABC = \frac{1}{2} \angle AOC = \frac{1}{2}\, \overset{\frown}{AC}
$$

Consequences:

- inscribed angles over the same arc are equal;
- an inscribed angle over a diameter - an angle in a semicircle - is
  a right angle;
- conversely, if an inscribed angle is right, its chord is a diameter.

## Worked Example

A central angle $\angle AOC = 100^\circ$. Find the inscribed angle $\angle
ABC$ over the same arc. Then: $AB$ is a diameter and $C$ is on the circle
- find $\angle ACB$. Then: inscribed angles $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ADC$
stand on the same arc and $\angle ABC = 37^\circ$ - find $\angle ADC$.

- **Half the central angle:** $\angle ABC = 50^\circ$.
- **Angle in a semicircle:** the arc $AB$ is half the circle, $180^\circ$;
  half of that is $90^\circ$. $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$.
- **Same arc, equal angles:** $\angle ADC = 37^\circ$.

Backwards: an inscribed angle of $65^\circ$ cuts off an arc of $130^\circ$,
and the central angle over it is $130^\circ$.

## Common Mistake

Halving the wrong angle, or forgetting whose vertex is where. The
*inscribed* angle is the small one, half the *central* angle - not the
other way round. And "same arc" matters: two inscribed angles on
*different* arcs of the same chord (one on each side) aren't equal,
they add to $180^\circ$. Check which arc each angle stands on before
equating.

## The Takeaway

A central angle measures its arc; an inscribed angle is half the arc
it subtends. So inscribed angles on the same arc are equal, and an
angle in a semicircle is right - which is also a test for a diameter.

## Check Yourself

1. A central angle is $84^\circ$. Find an inscribed angle on the same arc.
2. An inscribed angle is $28^\circ$. Find the arc it subtends.
3. $AB$ is a diameter, $C$ on the circle. Find $\angle ACB$.
4. Inscribed angles on the same arc are $x$ and $2x - 30^\circ$. Find $x$.
5. A triangle is inscribed in a circle and one side is a diameter.
   What kind of triangle?
6. Someone finds a central angle of $50^\circ$ from an inscribed angle of
   $100^\circ$ on the same arc. Which is which?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $42^\circ$.
> 2. $56^\circ$.
> 3. $90^\circ$.
> 4. $x = 2x - 30$, $x = 30^\circ$.
> 5. Right.
> 6. The central angle is twice the inscribed one: $100^\circ$ central
>    means $50^\circ$ inscribed, not the reverse.
