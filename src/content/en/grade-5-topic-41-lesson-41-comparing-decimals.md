## Why It Matters

Two runners clock $9.58$ and $9.6$ seconds - who won? Two jars of the same
jam cost $2.45$ and $2.5$ - which is cheaper? The second number in each
pair is shorter, and a shorter number *looks* smaller. It isn't. Comparing
decimals is nearly the same as comparing natural numbers, but "nearly"
hides one trap, and this topic is about not falling into it.

## The Big Idea

With natural numbers, more digits meant a bigger number, because every
extra digit adds a jar on the *left* - a bigger jar. With decimals, extra
digits go on the *right*, into smaller and smaller jars, and they can't
outweigh a single marble in a bigger jar. $9.6$ has six tenths; $9.58$ has
five tenths and some hundredths - and no number of hundredths can make up
a missing tenth.

So the digit count doesn't decide. What decides is the same thing it
always was: read from the biggest jar down, and the first place where the
numbers differ settles it. A trailing zero costs nothing to add - $9.6 =
9.60$ - and once both numbers have the same number of decimal places,
they compare like naturals.

## The Rule

To compare two decimals:

1. **Compare the whole parts.** The bigger whole part means the bigger
   number.
2. **If the whole parts are equal**, add zeros on the right so both have
   the same number of decimal places, then compare the fractional parts
   digit by digit from the left - tenths first, then hundredths, and so
   on.

Adding or removing zeros at the end of the fractional part never changes
a decimal: $9.6 = 9.60 = 9.600$.

On the coordinate ray decimals sit where their fractions would, and the
one further right is bigger.

## Worked Example

Which is bigger, $9.58$ or $9.6$?

- **Whole parts:** $9$ and $9$ - equal. Go to the fractions.
- **Same number of places:** $9.6$ becomes $9.60$.
- **Compare tenths:** $5$ against $6$. $6$ is bigger, and that decides it.

So $9.6 > 9.58$. The runner with $9.58$ was faster - the smaller time
wins.

Now order $0.7$, $0.68$, $0.702$, $0.7$ from smallest to largest. Pad to
three places: $0.700$, $0.680$, $0.702$, $0.700$. Now compare like
naturals: $680 < 700 = 700 < 702$. So $0.68 < 0.7 = 0.7 < 0.702$.

## Common Mistake

Deciding by length. "$9.58$ has more digits than $9.6$, so it's bigger" -
that's the natural-number rule applied where it doesn't belong. Extra
digits after the point are smaller pieces, not bigger jars. Pad with
zeros until the lengths match, then compare; or simply go place by
place from the left and stop at the first difference.

## The Takeaway

Compare the whole parts first; if they tie, compare tenths, then
hundredths, then thousandths. Trailing zeros are free, so pad the shorter
number - the number of decimal places never decides on its own.

## Check Yourself

1. Put in $<$, $>$ or $=$: $3.47$ and $3.5$.
2. Put in $<$, $>$ or $=$: $0.8$ and $0.80$.
3. Put in $<$, $>$ or $=$: $12.09$ and $12.1$.
4. Order from largest to smallest: $5.3$, $5.03$, $5.33$, $5.303$.
5. Which is cheaper, jam at $2.45$ or at $2.5$?
6. Someone says $0.125 > 0.3$ "because $125 > 3$." What's the mistake?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $3.47 < 3.5$ - tenths $4 < 5$.
> 2. Equal.
> 3. $12.09 < 12.1$ - tenths $0 < 1$.
> 4. $5.33 > 5.303 > 5.3 > 5.03$.
> 5. $2.45$ - it's $2.45 < 2.50$.
> 6. Pad first: $0.125$ and $0.300$; $125 < 300$, so $0.125 < 0.3$.
