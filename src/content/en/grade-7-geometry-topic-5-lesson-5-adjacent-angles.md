## Why It Matters

Draw a line and a ray sticking out of a point on it. Two angles
appear, one on each side of the ray, and they always add up to a
straight angle - $180^\circ$. That's the first theorem you'll prove in
full, and it's used everywhere: whenever a line is crossed, whenever
a triangle's side is extended, whenever two angles share a side.
Small as it is, most of what comes next stands on it.

## The Big Idea

Two angles are adjacent if they share a side and their other two
sides run in opposite directions along one line. Picture an open
book lying flat: the two covers meet at the spine, and the spine's two
halves point opposite ways. Because the outer sides form a straight
angle, and the shared side runs inside it, the additive property of
angle measure says the two angles sum to $180^\circ$. That *is* the
proof - two lines, straight from the axioms.

From it: if one adjacent angle is right, so is the other; if one is
acute, the other is obtuse; equal adjacent angles are both $90^\circ$.

## The Rule

Two angles are **adjacent** if they have one common side and their
other two sides are complementary rays.

**Theorem.** The sum of adjacent angles is $180^\circ$.

*Proof.* Let $\angle AOC$ and $\angle COB$ be adjacent: $OA$ and $OB$ are
complementary rays, so $\angle AOB$ is a straight angle, $180^\circ$. The
ray $OC$ lies between $OA$ and $OB$, so by the angle-measure axiom
$\angle AOC + \angle COB = \angle AOB = 180^\circ$. $\square$

Consequences:

- if two adjacent angles are equal, each is $90^\circ$;
- the angle adjacent to a right angle is right; to an acute one,
  obtuse; to an obtuse one, acute.

## Worked Example

One of two adjacent angles is $4$ times the other. Find them. Then: the
angle adjacent to $\angle AOB$ is $37^\circ$; find $\angle AOB$.

- **Let the smaller be $x$;** the larger is $4x$.
- **Adjacent angles sum to $180^\circ$:** $x + 4x = 180$, $5x = 180$, $x = 36$.
- **Answer:** $36^\circ$ and $144^\circ$. Check: they sum to $180$.
- **Second:** $\angle AOB = 180^\circ - 37^\circ = 143^\circ$.

A proof-style question: two adjacent angles are in the ratio $1 : 3$ -
what kind is each? $x + 3x = 180$, $x = 45$: $45^\circ$ (acute) and
$135^\circ$ (obtuse).

## Common Mistake

Calling any two angles with a common side "adjacent." Two angles
sharing a side whose *other* sides are not complementary rays - say,
$\angle AOC = 30^\circ$ and $\angle COB = 50^\circ$ with $\angle AOB = 80^\circ$ -
are just neighbouring angles, and they sum to whatever they sum to.
Adjacent needs the outer sides to make a straight line. Check the
line before using $180^\circ$.

## The Takeaway

Adjacent angles share a side and their other sides form a line; they
add to $180^\circ$ - proved straight from the axioms. An angle and its
adjacent angle determine each other, and if one is right, so is the
other.

## Check Yourself

1. Find the angle adjacent to $115^\circ$. To $90^\circ$.
2. Two adjacent angles differ by $50^\circ$. Find them.
3. Two adjacent angles are in the ratio $2 : 7$. Find them.
4. Can two adjacent angles both be acute? Both obtuse?
5. $\angle AOC$ and $\angle COB$ are adjacent, and $OD$ bisects $\angle AOC$
   with $\angle AOD = 25^\circ$. Find $\angle COB$.
6. Someone says $\angle AOC = 30^\circ$ and $\angle COB = 50^\circ$ are
   adjacent because they share $OC$. What's missing?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $65^\circ$; $90^\circ$.
> 2. $65^\circ$ and $115^\circ$.
> 3. $40^\circ$ and $140^\circ$.
> 4. No; no - the sum would be under or over $180^\circ$.
> 5. $\angle AOC = 50^\circ$, so $\angle COB = 130^\circ$.
> 6. Their outer sides $OA$ and $OB$ must be complementary rays; here they
>    aren't, since the angles don't sum to $180^\circ$.
