## Why It Matters

Every map app, every game engine, every graph you've ever drawn
works because a point can be named by two numbers. Grade 9 geometry
opens by taking that idea - the coordinate plane of grade 6 - and
turning it into a *method*: lines become equations, distances become
formulas, and a theorem about a triangle can be checked by
arithmetic. This topic sets the stage; the ones after it do the
computing.

## The Big Idea

Two perpendicular number lines through a common origin turn the
plane into a grid, and every point gets an address $(x; y)$: how far
along, how far up. The two axes split the plane into four quadrants,
and the *signs* of the coordinates say which quadrant a point is in.
Points on the axes have a zero coordinate. Symmetric points have
related coordinates: reflect over the $x$-axis and $y$ changes sign;
over the $y$-axis and $x$ does; through the origin and both do. All of
this is the vocabulary in which the next topics write geometry as
algebra.

## The Rule

The **coordinate plane** is fixed by two perpendicular axes - the
$x$-axis (abscissa) and the $y$-axis (ordinate) - meeting at the
**origin** $O(0; 0)$, with a unit of length. Each point $A$ has
**coordinates** $(x; y)$: $x$ is the signed distance to the $y$-axis,
$y$ to the $x$-axis. Write $A(x; y)$.

- **Quadrants:** I - $x > 0, y > 0$; II - $x < 0, y > 0$; III - $x < 0, y <
  0$; IV - $x > 0, y < 0$.
- A point on the $x$-axis has $y = 0$; on the $y$-axis, $x = 0$.
- **Symmetry:** $A(x; y)$ reflected in the $x$-axis is $(x; -y)$; in the
  $y$-axis, $(-x; y)$; in the origin, $(-x; -y)$.
- A **figure** is described by an equation or inequality in $x, y$ that
  its points satisfy: the line $y = 2$, the strip $-1 < x < 1$.

## Worked Example

Plot $A(3; 2)$, $B(-2; 4)$, $C(-3; -1)$, $D(4; -3)$ and name their
quadrants. Find the points symmetric to $A$ in each axis and in the
origin. Which of $P(0; 5)$, $Q(-4; 0)$ lie on an axis? Describe the set
of points with $y = -1$.

- **Quadrants:** $A$ - I; $B$ - II; $C$ - III; $D$ - IV. (Check the signs.)
- **Symmetries of $A(3; 2)$:** in the $x$-axis $(3; -2)$; in the $y$-axis
  $(-3; 2)$; in the origin $(-3; -2)$.
- **On an axis:** $P$ has $x = 0$ - on the $y$-axis; $Q$ has $y = 0$ - on
  the $x$-axis.
- **$y = -1$:** every point whose ordinate is $-1$, whatever its
  abscissa - a horizontal line one unit below the $x$-axis.

A rectangle with vertices $(-2; 1)$, $(3; 1)$, $(3; 4)$, $(-2; 4)$: its
sides are parallel to the axes, with lengths $5$ (difference of
$x$'s) and $3$ (difference of $y$'s), and area $15$.

## Common Mistake

Swapping the coordinates - plotting $(3; 2)$ as three up and two
along. The first coordinate is always the horizontal one. And getting
symmetries backwards: reflecting in the $x$-axis keeps $x$ and flips $y$
- the point moves vertically across the horizontal axis. Say it as
"the axis I reflect in is the one whose coordinate stays".

## The Takeaway

$(x; y)$ - along, then up. Quadrants by sign; axes by a zero
coordinate; reflections flip the *other* coordinate. Sets of points
are written as conditions on $x$ and $y$ - the start of geometry by
algebra.

## Check Yourself

1. In which quadrant is $(-5; 2)$? $(1; -7)$?
2. Find the point symmetric to $(4; -6)$ in the $y$-axis; in the
   origin.
3. Which points lie on the $x$-axis: $(0; 3)$, $(-2; 0)$, $(0; 0)$?
4. Describe the set of points with $x = 3$.
5. The points $(1; 2)$ and $(5; 2)$ are two vertices of a square with
   sides parallel to the axes. Give the other two (one solution).
6. Someone reflects $(2; 5)$ in the $x$-axis and gets $(-2; 5)$. Correct
   it.

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. II; IV.
> 2. $(-4; -6)$; $(-4; 6)$.
> 3. $(-2; 0)$ and $(0; 0)$.
> 4. A vertical line through $3$ on the $x$-axis.
> 5. Side $4$: $(1; 6)$ and $(5; 6)$ (or $(1; -2)$, $(5; -2)$).
> 6. $(2; -5)$ - the $x$-axis reflection flips $y$.
