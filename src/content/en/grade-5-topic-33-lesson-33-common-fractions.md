## Why It Matters

Half an hour. A quarter of a pizza. Three-fifths of the class voted yes.
The natural numbers count whole things, and they're stuck the moment
something is cut: how many pizzas is one slice? Fractions are the numbers
for parts of a whole, and they're the first new kind of number you meet
after the ones you count with.

## The Big Idea

Cut a chocolate bar into $8$ equal pieces and eat $3$. The bottom number
of the fraction $\frac{3}{8}$ says how many pieces the whole bar was cut
into - it names the *size* of a piece. The top number says how many of
those pieces you're talking about - it *counts* them. Read it from the
bottom up: "eighths" is the kind of thing, "three" is how many of them.

That's why $\frac{3}{8}$ isn't two numbers stuck together; it's one
number, and it has a place on the number line. Split the unit segment
into $8$ equal parts and step along $3$ of them - that's where it sits,
between $0$ and $1$.

## The Rule

A **common fraction** $\frac{a}{b}$ is written with a **numerator** $a$
above the bar and a **denominator** $b$ below it:

$$
\frac{3}{8} \quad \text{numerator } 3, \text{ denominator } 8
$$

The denominator shows into how many equal parts the whole is divided;
the numerator shows how many of those parts are taken. The denominator
is a natural number, never zero.

To find a **fraction of a number**, divide by the denominator and
multiply by the numerator: $\frac{3}{8}$ of $40$ is $40 : 8 \cdot 3 = 15$.

To find a **number from its fraction**, go the other way - divide by the
numerator and multiply by the denominator: if $\frac{3}{8}$ of a number
is $15$, the number is $15 : 3 \cdot 8 = 40$.

On the coordinate ray, $\frac{a}{b}$ is reached by splitting the unit
segment into $b$ parts and taking $a$ of them.

## Worked Example

A class has $30$ pupils, and $\frac{2}{5}$ of them play football. How
many play football?

- **Read the denominator.** Fifths: the class is split into $5$ equal
  groups. One group is $30 : 5 = 6$ pupils.
- **Read the numerator.** Two of those groups: $6 \cdot 2 = 12$.

So $12$ pupils play football.

Now backwards. $\frac{3}{4}$ of a rope is $27$ m. How long is the rope?
Three quarters make $27$ m, so one quarter is $27 : 3 = 9$ m, and the
whole rope, four quarters, is $9 \cdot 4 = 36$ m.

## Common Mistake

Multiplying and dividing in the wrong order - or rather, doing the wrong
one. To find $\frac{2}{5}$ of $30$, some people compute $30 \cdot 5 : 2 =
75$, which is bigger than the whole class. A fraction less than $1$ makes
a number smaller, so the answer must be below $30$. "Of a number" means
divide by the bottom and multiply by the top; "the number from its
fraction" is the reverse. A quick size check catches the swap every time.

## The Takeaway

A fraction is one number naming a part of a whole: the denominator says
how finely the whole is cut, the numerator says how many pieces. A
fraction of a number is "divide by the bottom, multiply by the top"; the
number from its fraction is the reverse.

## Check Yourself

1. Name the numerator and denominator of $\frac{7}{12}$. What does each
   mean?
2. Find $\frac{5}{6}$ of $42$.
3. $\frac{2}{7}$ of a number is $18$. Find the number.
4. A pizza is cut into $8$ slices and $5$ are eaten. What fraction is
   eaten? What fraction is left?
5. Mark $\frac{3}{4}$ on a coordinate ray whose unit segment is $4$
   squares long. How many squares from $0$ is it?
6. Someone finds $\frac{2}{5}$ of $30$ as $75$. Why is that impossible,
   and what's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. Numerator $7$ - how many parts are taken; denominator $12$ - into how
>    many parts the whole is cut.
> 2. $42 : 6 \cdot 5 = 35$.
> 3. $18 : 2 \cdot 7 = 63$.
> 4. $\frac{5}{8}$ eaten, $\frac{3}{8}$ left.
> 5. $3$ squares.
> 6. Two-fifths of something is less than the whole $30$; the answer is
>    $30 : 5 \cdot 2 = 12$.
