## Why It Matters

Fold a sheet of paper in half, then in half again, and again. After one
fold it's $2$ layers thick, after two folds $4$, after three $8$ - each fold
doubles it. After ten folds it would be $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2
\cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2$ layers. Nobody wants to write that,
let alone read it. Powers are the short way to write "the same number
multiplied by itself, this many times" - and they show up wherever things
double, square up or grow fast.

## The Big Idea

Multiplication was a shortcut for adding the same number over and over.
A power is the very same shortcut, one floor up: multiplying the same
number over and over. $2^{10}$ just means "ten twos, all multiplied
together." The little raised number counts the factors - nothing more.

Two powers have names of their own, and both come from pictures. A square
with side $5$ holds $5 \cdot 5 = 25$ small squares - so $5 \cdot 5$ is
called "five squared." A cube with edge $5$ holds $5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125$
small cubes - so that's "five cubed."

## The Rule

The **power** $a^n$ is the product of $n$ factors, each equal to $a$:

$$
a^n = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdot \dots \cdot a}_{n \text{ factors}}
$$

Here $a$ is the **base** - the number being multiplied - and $n$ is the
**exponent** - how many times it appears. Read $a^n$ as "$a$ to the
power $n$." For example:

$$
2^{10} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2
\cdot 2 = 1024
$$

The second power is the **square** of a number, $a^2 = a \cdot a$, and the
third is its **cube**, $a^3 = a \cdot a \cdot a$. The first power is the
number itself: $a^1 = a$.

Two families worth remembering. Powers of ten count zeros: $10^2 = 100$,
$10^3 = 1000$, $10^6 = 1\,000\,000$ - the exponent is how many zeros
follow the $1$. And $1$ to any power is still $1$, since multiplying ones
never gets you anywhere.

## Worked Example

Find $3^4$ and $7^2$.

- **Read the exponent.** $3^4$ means four threes multiplied together - not
  three times four.
- **Write them out:** $3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$.
- **Multiply left to right:** $3 \cdot 3 = 9$, $9 \cdot 3 = 27$,
  $27 \cdot 3 = 81$. So $3^4 = 81$.
- **The square** $7^2$ is $7 \cdot 7 = 49$.

Now the paper. After ten folds it's $2^{10} = 1024$ layers thick - over a
thousand sheets, which is why the tenth fold never happens.

## Common Mistake

Multiplying the base by the exponent. $3^4$ is not $3 \cdot 4 = 12$; it's
$3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 81$. The exponent doesn't join the
multiplication - it only says how many copies of the base do. Whenever a
power looks confusing, write out the factors and count them.

## The Takeaway

A power is a stack of identical factors written short: the base is the
factor, the exponent counts how many. The square is two factors, the cube
is three.

## Check Yourself

1. Find $2^5$, $5^3$ and $10^4$.
2. Write $6 \cdot 6 \cdot 6 \cdot 6 \cdot 6$ as a power. Name its base and
   exponent.
3. Which is bigger, $2^3$ or $3^2$?
4. Find the square of $12$ and the cube of $4$.
5. A square tile has side $9$ cm. How many $1$-cm squares fit in it? Write
   the answer as a power first.
6. Someone says $5^3 = 15$. What did they do, and what's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $32$, $125$ and $10\,000$.
> 2. $6^5$; the base is $6$, the exponent is $5$.
> 3. $3^2 = 9$ is bigger than $2^3 = 8$.
> 4. $12^2 = 144$ and $4^3 = 64$.
> 5. $9^2 = 81$ squares.
> 6. They multiplied $5 \cdot 3$. The power means $5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125$.
