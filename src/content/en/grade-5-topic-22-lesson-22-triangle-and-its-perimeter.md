## Why It Matters

Roof trusses, bicycle frames, bridge girders, the little bracket holding
up a shelf - they're all triangles, and for a reason: a triangle is the
only shape with straight sides that can't be pushed out of shape. Squash
a square frame and it leans into a diamond; a triangle just refuses. It's
the simplest closed shape there is, and the one everything else is cut
into.

## The Big Idea

Three points not in a row, three segments joining them - that's a
triangle. The three points are its corners, the segments its sides, and
walking all the way round it, along every side and back to where you
started, is its perimeter. It's the length of the fence, if the triangle
were a field.

Triangles come in kinds two different ways, because a triangle has two
kinds of parts. Look at the sides: are any equal? Look at the angles: is
one of them right, or obtuse? Each question sorts triangles on its own,
and one triangle has an answer to both.

## The Rule

A **triangle** $ABC$ is made of three points $A$, $B$, $C$ not on one
line - its **vertices** - and the three segments $AB$, $BC$, $CA$ joining
them - its **sides**. It has three **angles**: $\angle A$, $\angle B$,
$\angle C$.

The **perimeter** is the sum of the sides:

$$
P = AB + BC + CA
$$

By sides:

- **scalene** - all three sides different;
- **isosceles** - two sides equal (the third is the **base**);
- **equilateral** - all three sides equal.

By angles:

- **acute** - all three angles acute;
- **right** - one angle is right;
- **obtuse** - one angle is obtuse.

A triangle can have only one right or obtuse angle - two of them would
already be $180^\circ$ or more, leaving nothing for the third.

## Worked Example

A triangle has sides $7$ cm, $7$ cm and $10$ cm. Find its perimeter and
name its kind by sides.

- **Add the sides:** $P = 7 + 7 + 10 = 24$ cm.
- **Look for equal sides.** Two sides are $7$ cm - equal - and the third
  is different. So it's isosceles, with base $10$ cm.

Now the other way round. An isosceles triangle has perimeter $32$ cm and
base $12$ cm. Find the equal sides. The two equal sides together make
$32 - 12 = 20$ cm, so each is $20 : 2 = 10$ cm.

And by angles: a triangle with angles $30^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$
has one right angle - a right triangle. One with angles $50^\circ$,
$60^\circ$, $70^\circ$ is acute. One with an angle of $110^\circ$ is
obtuse.

## Common Mistake

Calling a triangle with two equal sides "not isosceles because the third
is different." Isosceles means *at least* two equal sides - the third
being different is exactly the usual case. In fact an equilateral triangle
is isosceles too, three times over. The definition asks for two equal
sides, and never that the third must differ.

## The Takeaway

A triangle is three vertices, three sides and three angles, and its
perimeter is the sides added up. By sides it's scalene, isosceles or
equilateral; by angles it's acute, right or obtuse - and every triangle
has one name from each list.

## Check Yourself

1. Find the perimeter of a triangle with sides $9$ cm, $12$ cm, $15$ cm.
2. An equilateral triangle has perimeter $27$ cm. Find its side.
3. A triangle has sides $5$ cm, $8$ cm, $5$ cm. Name it by sides. Which
   side is the base?
4. Name by angles a triangle with angles $40^\circ$, $50^\circ$,
   $90^\circ$.
5. Can a triangle have two obtuse angles? Why not?
6. An isosceles triangle has perimeter $50$ cm and each equal side is
   $18$ cm. Find the base.

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $9 + 12 + 15 = 36$ cm.
> 2. $27 : 3 = 9$ cm.
> 3. Isosceles; the base is the $8$ cm side.
> 4. Right.
> 5. No - two obtuse angles alone would add up to more than $180^\circ$.
> 6. $50 - 18 - 18 = 14$ cm.
