## Why It Matters

How far does a bicycle go in one turn of its wheel? How much fence
goes round a circular pond? How long is the rim of a clock face? Every
wheel, coin, plate and orbit is a circle, and the length of its rim
comes from one number that people have been chasing for four thousand
years - a number that begins $3.14$ and never ends.

## The Big Idea

Tie a pencil to a pin with a piece of string, pull it taut and swing it
all the way round: the pencil draws a circle. Every point of it is the
same distance - the length of the string - from the pin. That's the
whole definition: a circle is the set of points at one fixed distance
from a centre.

Now measure. Wrap a tape round any circle and compare with its
diameter: the rim is always a bit more than three diameters. Not
$3$ exactly, not $3.1$, not $3.14$ - the ratio is the same for every
circle in the world, and it's a number with infinitely many decimal
places, named $\pi$.

## The Rule

A **circle** is the set of all points in the plane at a given distance
from a given point. The point is the **centre** $O$; the distance is the
**radius** $r$. A segment from the centre to a point of the circle is
also called a radius; a segment through the centre with both ends on
the circle is a **diameter** $d$, and $d = 2r$. A segment joining any two
points of the circle is a **chord**; the diameter is the longest chord.

The **circumference** - the length of the circle - is proportional to
its diameter, and the coefficient is the number $\pi$:

$$
C = \pi d = 2\pi r, \qquad \pi \approx 3.14
$$

$\pi$ is an infinite non-repeating decimal, $3.14159\ldots$; in
calculations it's rounded, usually to $3.14$.

## Worked Example

A bicycle wheel has diameter $70$ cm. How far does the bicycle travel
in one turn of the wheel? In $100$ turns?

- **One turn is one circumference:** $C = \pi d \approx 3.14 \cdot 70 =
  219.8$ cm.
- **A hundred turns:** $219.8 \cdot 100 = 21\,980$ cm $\approx 220$ m.

Now backwards. A circular pond needs $47.1$ m of fence. What's its
radius? $C = 2\pi r$, so $r = C : (2\pi) \approx 47.1 : 6.28 = 7.5$ m.

## Common Mistake

Mixing up radius and diameter in the formula. With $r = 70$ cm, $C = \pi
d$ needs $d = 140$, not $70$ - or use $2\pi r$ directly. Half the correct
answer is the classic result of feeding a radius into a diameter
formula. Before computing, say which one you've been given.

## The Takeaway

A circle is all the points at one distance - the radius - from the
centre; the diameter is twice the radius. The circumference is $\pi$
times the diameter, and $\pi \approx 3.14$ is the same for every circle.

## Check Yourself

1. Find the circumference of a circle with diameter $10$ cm.
2. Find the circumference of a circle with radius $4.5$ m.
3. A circle has circumference $31.4$ cm. Find its diameter and radius.
4. A wheel of diameter $50$ cm turns $200$ times. How far has it rolled,
   in metres?
5. Can a chord be longer than the diameter? Why not?
6. Someone finds the circumference of a circle with radius $7$ as
   $3.14 \cdot 7 \approx 22$. What's the right answer?

> [!spoiler] Answers
>
> 1. $31.4$ cm.
> 2. $2 \cdot 3.14 \cdot 4.5 = 28.26$ m.
> 3. $d = 10$ cm, $r = 5$ cm.
> 4. $3.14 \cdot 50 \cdot 200 = 31\,400$ cm $= 314$ m.
> 5. No - the diameter passes through the centre and is the longest
>    chord.
> 6. $2 \cdot 3.14 \cdot 7 = 43.96$ - the formula with $d$ needs the
>    diameter, $14$.
